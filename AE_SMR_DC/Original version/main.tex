\begin{abstract}
% Applied Energy rule: single paragraph, <= 200 words. No "Here we show" framing.
% NOTE (target now Nature Energy): abstract revision deferred per author; the
% current text is the prior Applied Energy draft and will be rebuilt to the NE
% form (<= 150 words, unreferenced, "Here we" turn) in a later pass.
U.S. data centers are projected to consume up to 12\% of national electricity by 2028, increasing demand for firm low-carbon supply. Colocated small modular reactors (SMRs) with absorption cooling have been proposed as a waste-heat-use pathway, but their economics have rarely been tested with hourly cooling physics, multi-year prices and unit-size matching. We develop an hourly linear co-optimization for a 200~MW ERCOT data center, comparing grid supply, BWRX-300 generation, BWRX-300 with double-effect LiBr absorption cooling, and on-site natural gas combined cycle across 100 scenario runs. At 2023 ATB-Mid prices, only NGCC beats grid supply, by 17\%; SMR-only and SMR-absorption cost \$203~M/yr and \$207~M/yr versus \$64.3~M/yr for grid supply. A fixed BWRX-300 is poorly matched to the 95~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ average IT load: 2.5$\times$ load nearly eliminates merchant exports, but SMR-absorption remains 93\% above grid cost. At baseline load and zero carbon price, the published capital grid has no parity cell; the best NOAK/Bare-Low corner remains 10\% above grid cost. Under ATB-Mid capital assumptions, nuclear requires a carbon price near \$188/tCO$_2$ to beat grid procurement. Colocated SMR with absorption cooling is therefore a conditional, not default, low-carbon strategy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

U.S. data centers are reshaping electricity demand after a decade in which efficiency gains moderated global data-center energy growth \citep{masanet2020datacenter}. Consumption at U.S. data centers nearly doubled between 2019 and 2023 and is projected by the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory to reach 6.7\%--12\% of total U.S. electricity by 2028 \citep{shehabi2024}. The International Energy Agency projects global data-center electricity consumption to roughly double to 945~TWh by 2030, with the United States and China accounting for nearly 80\% of that growth \citep{iea2024electricity,iea2025energyAi}. The Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which serves a substantial share of the new U.S. data-center pipeline, expects its large-load interconnection queue to exceed 138~GW by 2030, creating a need for firm, dispatchable capacity beyond what variable renewables can supply alone \citep{ercot2025longterm}. The 2026 U.S. Annual Energy Outlook places much of this growth in regions managed by ERCOT and PJM, where transmission queues and capacity adequacy already constrain interconnection timelines \citep{eia2026aeo}.

Data-center loads impose an unusual set of requirements on the supplying generator. The workload is highly continuous, with capacity factors close to one, and modern hyperscale facilities require power availability at five nines or higher. Cooling is the dominant non-IT energy use, typically accounting for 20\%--40\% of total facility consumption, and it increases sharply with ambient wet-bulb temperature in inland sites such as Texas \citep{greenGrid2012pue,ashrae2021thermal}. These characteristics make data centers a natural counterpart to firm, low-carbon generators with baseload operating patterns. Small modular reactors (SMRs), in particular, have re-entered procurement and planning discussions because they provide dispatchable carbon-free output at unit sizes between roughly 50~MWe and 300~MWe, comparable to a single hyperscale campus \citep{mignacca2020smrFinance,doe2024liftoff,atb2024nuclear,asuega2023}.

Two streams of prior work converge on the system studied here. The first examines whether SMRs can supply data-center electricity at a cost competitive with grid supply. Asuega et al.~\citep{asuega2023} performed a Monte Carlo techno-economic analysis of light-water, gas-cooled, and molten-salt SMR configurations against natural gas combined cycle and conventional large reactors. They found that a 12~$\times$~77~MWe light-water SMR plant has a lower mean overnight capital cost and a shorter construction timeline than a single 1~GW reactor but remains above grid prices in most market regions without a carbon constraint. Recent nuclear-data-center studies extend this question to mixed SMR and distributed-energy portfolios, optimized cooling, and integrated power-thermal architectures \citep{debnath2025smrDER,you2025dynamicEquilibrium,bhowmik2026nuclearDC,zhang2026nuclearDC}. The Idaho National Laboratory meta-analysis of 48 advanced-reactor cost data sets reaches a similar conclusion, with capital cost estimates dispersed widely between first-of-a-kind and nth-of-a-kind cases \citep{inl2024metaanalysis}. Broader SMR finance reviews and nuclear construction-cost studies point to the same sensitivity to learning, standardization, project delivery, and financing structure \citep{mignacca2020smrFinance,nea2020constructionCost}. The U.S. Department of Energy Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Advanced Nuclear report consolidates this view and identifies long-term power purchase agreements with hyperscale technology firms as a financing mechanism that may lower effective capital cost over time through learning-by-doing \citep{doe2024liftoff}. Several 2024 and 2025 industry agreements between hyperscalers and reactor developers, including the Microsoft-Constellation Three Mile Island restart and the Google-Kairos Power 500~MW deal, reflect a shift from policy-driven to commercially driven nuclear deployment for data-center supply \citep{eia2024nuclearDC}. The 2024 NREL Annual Technology Baseline distinguishes 300~MWe SMR capital cost among first-of-a-kind, mid, and nth-of-a-kind trajectories, with the gap between the two extremes exceeding a factor of six in 2024 USD \citep{atb2024nuclear}.

The second stream studies cooling-side thermal integration. Reviews of data-center cooling technology identify low-grade waste heat as an underused resource but also emphasize that recovery value depends on sink temperature, operating hours, and auxiliary power \citep{ebrahimi2014review,yuan2023wasteHeatReview}. Thermodynamic and thermo-economic analyses of absorption chillers driven by data-center waste heat show that technically feasible cooling savings can be eroded by heat-exchanger approach temperatures, chiller capital cost, and low utilization during mild hours \citep{haywood2012thermodynamic,ebrahimi2015thermoeconomic}. Cooling consumes 20\% to 40\% of data-center energy in air-cooled facilities, and single-effect lithium-bromide water (LiBr-H$_2$O) absorption chillers convert low-grade heat into chilled water at coefficients of performance (COP) near 0.7 \citep{amiri2021}. Amiri et al.~\citep{amiri2021} reported annual energy savings of 4.3~GWh to 13.0~GWh for a 4.5~MW to 13.5~MW data center retrofitted with an absorption chiller driven by IT-side waste heat, with corresponding emissions savings of 3{,}068~tCO$_2$ to 9{,}208~tCO$_2$ per year. Cui et al.~\citep{cui2025switchable} proposed a switchable multistage absorption design targeting ultra-low-grade waste heat below conventional driving-temperature requirements. In the broader absorption-cooling literature, double-effect designs reach COP between 1.1 and 1.4 but require higher driving temperatures, typically above 130~$^\circ$C, that are more easily obtained from a steam-cycle reactor than from server-rack exhaust \citep{srikhirin2001absorption,herold2016absorption}. Across both streams, the absorption submodel is sensitive to ambient wet-bulb temperature: a higher cooling-water inlet temperature drives the LiBr solution toward the crystallization line, forcing a switch to vapor-compression backup \citep{cui2025switchable,herold2016absorption}. In Texas, this de-rating concentrates in July and August afternoons, when wet-bulb temperatures regularly exceed 26~$^\circ$C.

Adjacent co-location studies in renewable-hydrogen and transactive-energy systems provide methodological context for the boundary-analysis framing used here. Prior work has evaluated wind- and hydrogen-system co-location, Texas hydrogen decarbonization pathways, and facility--microgrid location-allocation for integrated energy operations \citep{li2024windHydrogen,li2025hydrogenTexas,li2023microgridLocation}. Those studies motivate the same emphasis on siting, market regime and infrastructure coupling, but they do not model a nuclear steam cycle coupled to data-center cooling. Recent demand-side work using real-time greenhouse-gas intensity also motivates the use of time-resolved carbon accounting rather than annual-average emission factors \citep{li2025ghgDemand}.

Together these two streams leave the siting and procurement decision underdetermined. Studies of waste-heat utilization through absorption cooling have largely treated it as a categorical benefit, comparing a no-recovery baseline against a fully integrated configuration without identifying the technology- and market-specific conditions under which integration actually pays. Hourly cooling physics, including wet-bulb-driven COP de-rating and crystallization downtime, has rarely been combined with multi-year market data spanning the recent range of Henry Hub gas prices, ERCOT locational marginal prices and reactor capital-cost trajectories. And because sensitivity analyses in this setting are usually one-dimensional, they offer no multi-axis envelope that maps a candidate site or financing structure onto a competitive or non-competitive region. Without such an envelope, claims about the viability of SMR with absorption cooling rest heavily on the chosen baseline year and reactor capital-cost path.

Here we present a techno-economic boundary analysis of a colocated SMR with double-effect absorption cooling, driven by cascaded HP--LP turbine extraction, for a 200~MW hyperscale data center on the ERCOT grid. A linear program co-optimizes annualized total cost across power generation, cooling supply and battery storage at hourly resolution over 8760-hour annual horizons, using year-resolved ERCOT day-ahead prices, Henry Hub gas prices and Houston wet-bulb temperatures from 2022 to 2024. We compare four configurations: grid-only supply with vapor-compression chillers (Case~0); a BWRX-300 plant with vapor-compression chillers (Case~1); a BWRX-300 plant coupled to a double-effect LiBr--H$_2$O absorption chiller driven by mid-pressure turbine extraction, with vapor-compression backup and a crystallization gate (Case~2); and an on-site natural gas combined cycle plant with vapor-compression chillers (Case~3). A multi-axis sensitivity design exposes each configuration to ranges in data-center load, power usage effectiveness, market year, battery deployment, reactor capital-cost trajectory, absorption-chiller capital cost, carbon price and cost of capital.

The analysis makes three advances. It couples an hourly absorption submodel, with wet-bulb-driven COP de-rating and a LiBr crystallization gate, to multi-year ERCOT price, Henry Hub and reactor-capital trajectories within a single co-optimization, and uses this to map the cost-competitiveness envelope of SMR with absorption cooling jointly over reactor and absorption-chiller capital cost. It separates this jointly tested capital-cost frontier from one-axis movements toward parity along the policy, financing, market-year and unit-size axes. And it converts the resulting envelope into procurement-relevant boundary conditions that generalize beyond ERCOT to other markets, climates and data-center sizes. Scenario definitions and input provenance are documented in the Supplementary Information.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the system configurations, the optimization formulation, the absorption submodel, and the data inputs. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results and discusses the boundary conditions for thermal integration and the implications for site selection and procurement. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{System configurations}
\label{sec:methods:system}

The analysis compares four configurations that supply electricity and chilled water to a single 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ rated hyperscale data center in Houston, Texas. Each configuration uses the same data-center load profile, vapor-compression chiller technology when required, and financial parameters; the configurations differ only in the electricity supply pathway and in whether the cooling chain uses recovered reactor heat. Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} summarizes the cogeneration mechanism, the grid and storage interfaces, and the boundary conditions varied in the sensitivity analysis. Cases~0, 1 and 3 are reduced versions of this layout in which selected blocks are disabled. Table~\ref{tab:cases} summarizes the four configurations.

Case~0 represents the conventional grid-supplied data center. All electricity is purchased from ERCOT at the day-ahead locational marginal price, and all cooling is produced by a water-cooled centrifugal vapor-compression chiller with a Houston-derated coefficient of performance of 3.2. This case serves as the benchmark for the heat-recovery premium reported throughout the paper. Case~1 replaces grid imports with on-site BWRX-300 generation while retaining the same VCC. Heat at the turbine condenser is rejected to the cooling tower and is not recovered. Bidirectional grid exchange is retained so that excess electricity can be sold to ERCOT during low-load hours and grid imports can cover residual demand during planned outages. Case~2 adds a double-effect LiBr--H$_2$O absorption chiller fed by a mid-pressure extraction tap on the high-pressure (HP) turbine. The cascade keeps the low-pressure (LP) turbine in service, so diverted steam incurs a Willans penalty rather than the full turbine efficiency penalty. A 100~MW$_\mathrm{c}$ vapor-compression backup chiller remains installed, compared with 120~MW$_\mathrm{c}$ in the primary-VCC cases, to cover hours when the absorption unit must shut down because the cooling-water inlet exceeds the LiBr crystallization threshold. Case~3 replaces the SMR with a 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ on-site F-class natural gas combined cycle, retains the VCC, and disconnects from the grid to isolate the value of replacing grid procurement with a fossil-fuelled on-site alternative.

The data-center IT load follows an annual hourly trace synthesized from a 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ hyperscale reference profile with a peak hour of 142.4~MW$_\mathrm{e}$, an annual average of 94.7~MW$_\mathrm{e}$, and an annual utilization of 0.67. The same trace is used in every case and every scenario; the IT load is not a decision variable. Cooling demand at hour $t$ is derived from the power usage effectiveness (PUE):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cool_demand}
Q_{cool}^{dem}(t) \;=\; \frac{(\mathrm{PUE} - 1)\, P_{IT}(t)}{\eta_{ch}},
\end{equation}
where $\eta_{ch}=0.90$ accounts for the chilled-water distribution chain. Baseline PUE is 1.30, consistent with standard data-center energy-efficiency reporting and recent benchmarks of operating air-cooled hyperscale facilities in inland Texas \citep{greenGrid2012pue,ashrae2021thermal}; the PUE sensitivity in Section~\ref{sec:results:pue} sweeps PUE over $\{1.10, 1.30, 1.50\}$.

\subsection{Optimization formulation}
\label{sec:methods:opt}

The four cases are formulated as separate dispatch problems on the same 8760-hour horizon. Cases~0 and 3 reduce to closed-form annual cost evaluations because the absence of storage and heat recovery makes the hourly decisions independent. Cases~1 and 2 are linear programs; binary variables are not introduced because round-trip losses in the battery block make simultaneous charge and discharge unprofitable. The same accounting structure is applied to all four cases so that cross-case comparisons remain on a common basis.

\runinhead{Reactor and turbine block (Cases~1 and 2)}
The BWRX-300 reactor is modelled as a continuous thermal source with bounded ramping and an annual capacity-factor constraint. The reactor block enforces
\begin{align}
\underline{P}_{rx} \;\le\; & P_{rx}(t) \;\le\; \overline{P}_{rx}, &\forall\, t \in \mathcal{T}, \label{eq:rx_bounds}\\
\big|P_{rx}(t) - P_{rx}(t-1)\big| \;\le\; & \Delta P_{rx}^{\max}, &\forall\, t \ge 1, \label{eq:rx_ramp}\\
\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} P_{rx}(t) \;\ge\; & \overline{\mathrm{CF}} \cdot \overline{P}_{rx} \cdot |\mathcal{T}|, & \label{eq:rx_cf}
\end{align}
with $\overline{P}_{rx}=870$~MW$_\mathrm{th}$, $\underline{P}_{rx}=435$~MW$_\mathrm{th}$ (50\% minimum-load fraction from the BWRX-300 design basis), $\Delta P_{rx}^{\max}=522$~MW$_\mathrm{th}$/h corresponding to a 1\,\%/min ramp, and $\overline{\mathrm{CF}}=0.92$ from the mature U.S.\ light-water-reactor fleet average \citep{gehitachi2024bwrx300}. The annual capacity-factor constraint sets an annual energy floor equivalent to roughly one month of downtime, without modelling the timing of individual outages or introducing unit-commitment binaries.

The Rankine steam cycle is represented by a cascaded HP--LP turbine with a Willans-line extraction penalty. Letting $\eta_r=0.345$ be the rated electric efficiency at zero extraction and $\alpha_w=0.083$~MW$_\mathrm{e}$/MW$_\mathrm{th}$ the marginal electricity lost per MW$_\mathrm{th}$ of mid-pressure steam diverted to the absorber:
\begin{align}
P_{tg}(t) \;=\; & \eta_r\, P_{rx}(t) \;-\; \alpha_w\, Q_{ext}(t), \label{eq:turb_willans}\\
P_{tn}(t) \;=\; & (1 - \xi_{aux})\, P_{tg}(t), \label{eq:turb_aux}
\end{align}
with $\xi_{aux}=0.10$. Because diverted steam still expands through the HP turbine before extraction, the Willans coefficient is roughly one order of magnitude smaller than charging the full turbine efficiency against extracted heat, as would be appropriate for a parallel main-steam tap.

\runinhead{Absorption-chiller submodel (Case~2)}
The double-effect LiBr--H$_2$O absorption chiller is described by a time-varying coefficient of performance and a binary availability indicator that captures LiBr crystallization risk \citep{srikhirin2001absorption,herold2016absorption}. With $T_{wb,0}=26\,^\circ$C as the Houston design wet-bulb, $\mathrm{COP}_a^{\,0}=1.10$ as the COP at the design wet-bulb, $\delta=0.015\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ as the linear de-rating slope, and $\overline{\mathrm{COP}}_a=1.30$ as the nameplate cap, the chiller COP at wet-bulb $T_{wb}(t)$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cop_abs}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{COP}_a\!\big(T_{wb}(t)\big) =
\max\Big\{&
  \min\big[\mathrm{COP}_a^{\,0}
  - \delta\,(T_{wb}(t)-T_{wb,0}), \\
& \overline{\mathrm{COP}}_a\big],\; 0.5\Big\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The lower bound of 0.5 is a numerical safeguard; Houston wet-bulbs in the weather record do not reduce a physical chiller to this value because the crystallization gate engages first. With $\Delta T_{cw}=5$~K as the cooling-tower approach and $T_{cry}=32\,^\circ$C as the LiBr-crystallization-line cooling-water inlet, the availability indicator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abs_avail}
a(t) \;=\; \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } T_{wb}(t) + \Delta T_{cw} \le T_{cry}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}
\end{equation}
so the absorption chiller is forced offline whenever the cooling-water inlet would exceed the LiBr crystallization line. Equivalently, with the $\Delta T_{cw}=5$~K tower approach this is a wet-bulb gate at $T_{cry}-\Delta T_{cw}=32-5=27\,^\circ$C, the value marked in Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}a. Delivered absorption cooling is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abs_yield}
Q_{a}(t) \;=\; \mathrm{COP}_a\!\big(T_{wb}(t)\big)\cdot a(t)\cdot Q_{ext}(t),
\end{equation}
and a parasitic electric load $\beta_a\, Q_a(t)$ with $\beta_a=0.020$~kW$_\mathrm{e}$/kW$_\mathrm{c}$ powers solution and refrigerant pumps. The vapor-compression backup carries the residual cooling load whenever $a(t)=0$.

\runinhead{Cooling, BESS and grid blocks}
Vapor-compression cooling links to its electric input through a fixed Houston-derated COP, $Q_{v}(t)=\mathrm{COP}_v\, P_v(t)$, with $\mathrm{COP}_v=3.2$. The lithium-ion BESS, sized at 100~MWh / 50~MW (C/2), follows the standard state-of-charge dynamics
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bess_soc}
E_B(t) \;=\; (1-\rho_B)\,E_B(t-1) + \big(\sqrt{\eta_{rt}}\,B^{+}(t) - B^{-}(t)/\sqrt{\eta_{rt}}\big)\Delta t,
\end{equation}
with cyclical closure $E_B(|\mathcal{T}|) = E_B(0)$, $\eta_{rt}=0.85$, $\rho_B=4.17\times10^{-5}$\,h$^{-1}$, and SOC bounds $[0.05,\, 0.95]\,\mathrm{Cap}_E$. Grid exchange is bounded by the 300~MW point of common coupling, $0 \le P_g^{+}(t),\, P_g^{-}(t) \le \overline{P}_{pcc}$.

\runinhead{Energy balances}
Two equality constraints hold every hour. The electric balance equates supply, BESS discharge and grid imports to the data-center IT load, chiller drives, absorber parasitics, BESS charge and grid exports:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:elec_balance}
P_{tn}(t) + P_g^{+}(t) + B^{-}(t)
\;=\; & P_{IT}(t) + P_v(t) + \beta_a Q_a(t) \notag\\
\;+\; & P_g^{-}(t) + B^{+}(t).
\end{align}
The cooling balance equates total cooling supplied to the cooling demand of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cool_demand}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cool_balance}
Q_a(t) + Q_v(t) \;=\; Q_{cool}^{dem}(t).
\end{equation}
For Cases~0, 1 and 3 the absorption variables $Q_a(t)$, $Q_{ext}(t)$ are fixed to zero; for Case~3 the reactor and turbine variables are removed and the IT and chiller load is served entirely by the NGCC block at a constant heat rate $\eta_{hhv}=0.495$.

\runinhead{Objective}
The optimization minimizes total annualized cost (TAC) summed over six accounting buckets:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tac}
\begin{aligned}
\min\; \mathrm{TAC} ={}&
\mathrm{CAPEX}\cdot\mathrm{CRF} + \mathrm{FOM} + \mathrm{VOM}\\
& + \Phi_{fuel} + \Phi_{grid} + \Phi_{co_2},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CAPEX}=\sum_k \mathrm{CAPEX}_k S_k$ aggregates overnight capital across reactor, turbine, absorption and VCC chillers, BESS and NGCC sized at $S_k$; $\mathrm{FOM}=\sum_k \mathrm{FOM}_k S_k$ aggregates the fixed O\&M; the variable terms are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{VOM} ={}&
  \sum_t \big[v_{rx} P_{tn}(t) + v_v Q_v(t) + v_a Q_a(t)\big]\Delta t
  \notag\\
& + \sum_t v_B\big(B^{+}(t)+B^{-}(t)\big)\Delta t, \label{eq:vom}\\
\Phi_{fuel} ={}& \pi_f \sum_t P_{rx}(t)\Delta t, \label{eq:fuel_cost}\\
\Phi_{grid} ={}&
  \sum_t \pi_g(t)\big[P_g^{+}(t) - P_g^{-}(t)\big]\Delta t, \label{eq:grid_cost}\\
\Phi_{co_2} ={}&
  \pi_{co_2}\Theta \sum_t
  \Big[\gamma_{rx} P_{tn}(t) + \theta(t)\big(P_g^{+}(t)-P_g^{-}(t)\big)\Big]\Delta t. \label{eq:carbon_cost}
\end{align}
For Case~3, the same accounting buckets are used but the reactor terms are replaced by the NGCC terms. The VOM term uses $v_{\mathrm{ng}}P_{\mathrm{ng}}(t)+v_vQ_v(t)$, where $P_{\mathrm{ng}}(t)$ is the NGCC electric output required by the IT and VCC loads, and the fuel and carbon terms become
\begin{align}
\Phi_{fuel}^{ng} ={}& \sum_t \pi_{\mathrm{ng}}(t)\,h_{\mathrm{ng}}\,P_{\mathrm{ng}}(t)\Delta t, \label{eq:ngcc_fuel_cost}\\
\Phi_{co_2}^{ng} ={}&
  \pi_{co_2}\Theta \sum_t
  \gamma_{\mathrm{ng}} P_{\mathrm{ng}}(t)\Delta t, \label{eq:ngcc_carbon_cost}
\end{align}
with $h_{\mathrm{ng}}=3.412/\eta_{hhv}$~MMBtu/MWh$_\mathrm{e}$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{ng}}=420$~g\,CO$_2$-eq/kWh$_\mathrm{e}$, equal to 360~g/kWh$_\mathrm{e}$ direct combustion emissions plus 60~g/kWh$_\mathrm{e}$ upstream methane CO$_2$-equivalent. The carbon term $\Phi_{co_2}$ multiplies the case carbon footprint by a uniform price $\pi_{co_2}$ and a unit-conversion factor $\Theta=10^{-3}$ tCO$_2$/kg. Exports credit the case at the ERCOT hourly average carbon intensity $\theta(t)$ to reflect the time-varying emissions of the grid power exchanged, following time-resolved hourly emissions accounting rather than a static annual-average factor \citep{li2025ghgDemand}; this hourly average emission factor is used as a tractable proxy for the true marginal emission factor \citep{hawkes2010marginal,silerEvans2013regional}. The capital recovery factor is $\mathrm{CRF}=i(1+i)^{N}/[(1+i)^{N}-1]$ with project life $N=20$~yr and baseline WACC $i=6.7\%$; the WACC sensitivity in Section~\ref{sec:results:wacc} sweeps $i$ over $\{5\%, 6.7\%, 10\%\}$.

The cross-case figure of merit used throughout the rest of the paper is the \emph{heat-recovery premium}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:premium}
\mathrm{HRP}_c \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\mathrm{TAC}_c}{\mathrm{TAC}_0}\Big|_{\text{matched scenario}},
\end{equation}
defined as one minus the ratio of the case TAC to the Case~0 TAC at the same year, PUE and carbon price. A positive HRP means the case beats the grid-only baseline, and its magnitude is the relative cost difference. Although named for the cogeneration motivation of Case~2, the premium is a general measure of cost competitiveness against grid procurement; we report it for all four configurations, including those that recover no heat.

\label{sec:methods:data}

All runs use 8760-hour annual operating horizons; leap-year raw files are trimmed to 8760 hourly records for cross-year comparability. The four characteristic input traces are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}; aggregated values and source citations are listed in Tables~\ref{tab:data} and~\ref{tab:equipment}.

The Houston wet-bulb temperature distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}a) drives the absorption-chiller availability constraint in Equation~\eqref{eq:abs_avail}. Monthly box plots from the Open-Meteo historical-weather archive for the 2023 calendar year show that wet-bulb exceeds 26~$^\circ$C (the absorption design anchor $T_{wb,0}$) in about 32\% of summer hours and crosses the LiBr crystallization gate at 27~$^\circ$C in about 5\% of summer hours, concentrated in July and August afternoons. The ERCOT day-ahead LMP duration curves (Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}b) capture the recent range of grid-procurement cost: 2022 represents the high natural-gas price regime, with an annual mean LMP of \$70/MWh$_\mathrm{e}$ and frequent excursions above \$200/MWh$_\mathrm{e}$; 2023 was a moderate year at \$57/MWh$_\mathrm{e}$; and 2024 declined to \$28/MWh$_\mathrm{e}$ as gas prices receded and renewable generation added supply. The IT-load profile (Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}c) follows a typical hyperscale shape with mild diurnal seasonality and an annual capacity factor of 0.67; peak load reaches 142~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ and annual mean load is 95~MW$_\mathrm{e}$. The absorption-chiller COP$_a(T_{wb})$ curve (Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}d) parameterizes Equation~\eqref{eq:cop_abs}: COP decreases linearly from the nameplate cap of 1.30 at cool ambient wet-bulb to 1.10 at the design anchor of 26~$^\circ$C, after which the crystallization gate forces a binary cutoff and the VCC backup carries the cooling load.

\subsection{Baseline economics and operational anatomy}
\label{sec:results:baseline}

Figure~\ref{fig:tac_stack} decomposes the annualized cost of the four configurations at the 2023 ATB-Mid baseline. The grid-supplied data center (Case~0) carries a TAC of \$64.3~M/yr, of which 94\% is the electricity bill at the ERCOT day-ahead price. The two SMR configurations carry much higher TAC values of \$202.8~M/yr (Case~1) and \$207.0~M/yr (Case~2), driven almost entirely by reactor capital recovery; revenue from selling surplus electricity to ERCOT offsets only about one third of the capital line. The on-site NGCC plant (Case~3) is the only on-site configuration that beats the grid at this baseline, at \$53~M/yr, or a heat-recovery premium of $+17\%$, because the 2023 Houston Ship Channel gas price of \$2.63/MMBtu held the annual on-site fuel cost below the annual grid-procurement bill. The day-ahead price still fell below the NGCC running cost of about \$21/MWh$_\mathrm{e}$ in roughly 42\% of hours, but the islanded Case~3 cannot buy grid power in those hours. Case~2 is \$4.2~M/yr more expensive than Case~1, equivalent to a 6.5~percentage-point penalty relative to Case~1 against the Case~0 baseline. Thus, under 2023 ATB-Mid prices, adding absorption cooling to the nuclear plant does not reduce data-center cost because the absorption capital cost outweighs the cooling-electricity savings.

\runinhead{Sizing and the overbuild component of the premium}
The premium reported here reflects the smallest commercial BWRX-300 unit (270~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ net at a 0.92 capacity factor), which is approximately 2.9 times the data center's 94.7~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ average IT load and 2.6 times the on-site electricity demand after cooling parasitics. The reactor consequently exports a structural surplus of about 1.3~TWh/yr to ERCOT. The S8 size-matching sweep in Figure~\ref{fig:size_matching} holds the BWRX-300 fixed while scaling the data-center load and data-center-side equipment from 0.5$\times$ to 3.0$\times$. Increasing the campus to 2.5$\times$ the baseline load, or 237~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ mean IT demand, nearly eliminates the net export surplus in Case~2 (0.043~TWh/yr net, against gross flows near 1.3~TWh/yr in each direction), yet the cogeneration case remains 93\% more expensive than the matching grid-only baseline. The annual dollar gap to grid supply is not eliminated: it is \$143~M/yr at 1.0$\times$, \$149~M/yr at 2.5$\times$ and \$151~M/yr at 3.0$\times$, because the larger campus also raises the matching grid-only counterfactual. What improves is utilization intensity: Case~2 TAC per MWh of IT load falls from \$250/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ at 1.0$\times$ to \$150/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ at 2.5$\times$ and \$138/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ at 3.0$\times$; the excess cost over grid falls from \$172/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ to \$72/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ and \$61/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ over the same range. At 3.0$\times$ load, Case~2 becomes a net importer (0.38~TWh/yr) and improves to a $-78\%$ premium, still far from parity. A continuous load-matched-reactor counterfactual (about 104~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ net, with reactor capital scaled in proportion) gives a similar residual premium of $-90\%$. Thus, unit-size indivisibility and merchant overbuild explain a large share of the 200~MW-campus result, but eliminating overbuild does not remove the underlying ATB-Mid reactor-capital penalty.

\runinhead{Operational anatomy of the cogeneration case}
\label{sec:results:dispatch}

The hourly dispatch of Case~2 shows how the absorption chiller integrates with the reactor under winter and summer operating conditions (Figure~\ref{fig:dispatch}). In the winter week, the BWRX-300 holds its annual capacity factor at 92\% of nameplate thermal output, the HP--LP turbine produces close to its full $\sim 270$~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ net output through most of the window (turning down to about 135~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ during the low-price hours), chilled-water demand is low (PUE\,1.30 against the 60--80~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ winter IT load), and the extraction tap delivers steam to the absorption chiller throughout the 168~hour window. The vapor-compression backup remains idle. In the July week, wet-bulb temperature climbs toward and past the 26~$^\circ$C design anchor on the warm afternoons, derating the absorption COP from its cap toward its 1.10 design value; it stays below the 27~$^\circ$C crystallization gate (32~$^\circ$C cooling-water inlet) throughout this week, so the absorption chiller carries the cooling load continuously and the vapor-compression backup remains idle. The crystallization gate engages only on the hottest summer afternoons elsewhere in the record: across the full year the availability indicator in Equation~\ref{eq:abs_avail} forces the absorption chiller offline for about 115~hours, concentrated in late June through August (annual peak wet-bulb 28~$^\circ$C), during which the VCC backup carries 4.1~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$ of cooling while the absorption chiller delivers 272~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$.

The waterfall in Figure~\ref{fig:value_decomp} decomposes the \$4.2~M/yr gap between Case~2 and Case~1 into the marginal value components associated with adding absorption cooling to the nuclear-only plant. The monetized direct-site-water increment is only \$0.01~M/yr, so it is retained for accounting completeness rather than because it materially affects the conclusion. A \$0.69~M/yr residual reconciles the proxy component sum with the measured TAC difference. The chiller value proposition is therefore primarily a CAPEX problem rather than an operating-physics or water-cost problem.

\subsection{Boundary-condition sensitivities}
\label{sec:results:sensitivities}

The sensitivities below translate the baseline cost penalty into a boundary map. Figure~\ref{fig:cooling_response} first isolates the cooling-side demand response and the marginal value of the absorption chiller. Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas} then collects the non-policy competitiveness boundaries in a single atlas: market year, reactor capital cost, financing and the joint reactor--absorber capital-cost frontier. This ordering separates one-axis movements toward parity from the two-axis capital-cost test that a procurement decision would require. Figure~\ref{fig:policy_sensitivity_summary} then asks whether a carbon price can move the nuclear configurations across the grid-only baseline and ranks the remaining one-variable sensitivities.

\runinhead{Cooling-load fraction (PUE sensitivity)}
\label{sec:results:pue}

The cooling-load fraction is set by the PUE. Figure~\ref{fig:cooling_response} sweeps PUE over $\{1.10, 1.30, 1.50\}$ for Cases~1 and 2 with all other parameters held at the 2023 ATB-Mid baseline, and it also places the same Case~2-minus-Case~1 heat-recovery-premium gap against the carbon-price axis. Higher PUE improves the absorption-chiller value proposition by increasing useful thermal-cooling duty: Case~2 absorption cooling rises from 91 to 272 and 454~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$/yr across the three PUE levels. The resulting Case~2 minus Case~1 TAC penalty narrows from \$6.9~M/yr at PUE\,1.10 to \$4.2~M/yr at PUE\,1.30 and \$1.5~M/yr at PUE\,1.50. The absorber therefore becomes more valuable at high cooling load, but the additional duty is still insufficient to make a \$750/kW$_\mathrm{c}$ absorber cheaper than the VCC-and-export alternative. In heat-recovery-premium terms, both higher PUE and higher carbon price reduce, but do not eliminate, the Case~2 penalty relative to Case~1.

\centering

(c) Case~2 minus Case~1 heat-recovery-premium gap across PUE. (d) The same premium gap across carbon price. Higher cooling load and carbon price narrow the cogeneration penalty, but neither removes it.}

\runinhead{Multi-year electricity price regime}
\label{sec:results:year}

The ERCOT price regime during the year of operation is the largest single market sensitivity (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}, panel a). The 2022 cluster captures the high natural-gas price regime: delivered natural gas (Henry Hub plus Houston Ship Channel basis) averaged \$6.53/MMBtu, and the NGCC case's premium falls to $-10\%$, essentially reaching parity with the grid because both the grid clearing price and the on-site fuel cost moved together. In 2023, low gas prices (\$2.63/MMBtu) combined with high ERCOT prices made Case~3 the only on-site option that beat grid supply ($+17\%$ premium). The 2024 cluster shows the deepest negative premium for every on-site option because ERCOT LMPs settled at a multi-year low ($\sim$\$28/MWh annual mean), making grid procurement comparatively inexpensive. Across the three years, no on-site option beats grid supply in 2024, only NGCC beats grid supply in 2023, and no on-site option beats grid supply in 2022. The year regime therefore sets the market boundary for the on-site gas option: a $\pm 50\%$ swing in Henry Hub gas price or ERCOT LMP can overturn the ranking between grid and NGCC supply. Nuclear, by contrast, stays far above grid cost in all three years, so its ranking is governed by the capital-cost rather than the market axis.

\runinhead{Battery storage}
\label{sec:results:bess}

The BESS axis tests whether a 100~MWh / 50~MW lithium-ion battery changes the competitiveness frontier. It lowers TAC by \$1.18~M/yr in each nuclear case, improving the premium by only 1.8 percentage points and never altering the configuration ranking (see Supplementary Information). Because this effect is much smaller than the market, capital-cost and financing axes, it is retained as a secondary boundary rather than as a main panel in Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}.

\runinhead{Reactor capital cost trajectory}
\label{sec:results:capex}

SMR overnight capital cost is the dominant technology-cost axis (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}, panel b). At FOAK (\$14{,}700/kW$_\mathrm{e}$), the premium is $-490\%$ (Case~1) and $-497\%$ (Case~2); reactor capital outweighs all other costs by a factor of five. At ATB-Mid the premium settles at $-216\%$ (Case~1) and $-222\%$ (Case~2). At NOAK (\$2{,}250/kW$_\mathrm{e}$), the premium rises to $-8\%$ for Case~1 and $-15\%$ for Case~2, indicating that the nuclear cases come within 8--15\% of cost parity with the grid even without thermal integration. NOAK is necessary but not sufficient: even at the lowest published capital trajectory neither nuclear case crosses the premium $=0$ line. Coupling the NOAK reactor with the absorption chiller (Case~2 NOAK) is \emph{less} competitive than Case~1 NOAK because the absorption capital still outweighs the cooling-side savings.

\runinhead{Cost of capital}
\label{sec:results:wacc}

The financing assumption changes the same capital-dominated boundary rather than creating a separate operating mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}, panel c). Sweeping the weighted-average cost of capital between 5\% and 10\% for Case~2 while holding absorption capital, reactor capital and operating parameters fixed moves TAC from \$182~M/yr to \$262~M/yr, an \$80~M/yr range. The implied heat-recovery premium ranges from $-182\%$ at 5\% WACC to $-307\%$ at 10\% WACC. By comparison, the full range of S4 (SMR CAPEX between FOAK and NOAK) moves the Case~2 premium between $-497\%$ and $-15\%$. Financing materially affects the high-cost FOAK--ATB region but matters less than the reactor capital-cost axis once the trajectory reaches NOAK. Policy or contracting mechanisms that lower the effective cost of capital for SMR projects, such as long-term offtake agreements, sovereign loan guarantees, or accelerated depreciation, can compress but not eliminate the gap to grid supply.

\runinhead{Joint SMR and absorption capital cost frontier}
\label{sec:results:viability}

The joint capital-cost frontier in Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}, panel d, turns the preceding one-axis sensitivities into the procurement-relevant capital-cost test. Both axes sweep simultaneously: SMR capital cost runs from NOAK \$2{,}250/kW$_\mathrm{e}$ to FOAK \$14{,}700/kW$_\mathrm{e}$ on the vertical axis, and absorption-chiller capital cost runs from Bare-Low \$450/kW$_\mathrm{c}$ to Turnkey-High \$1{,}200/kW$_\mathrm{c}$ on the horizontal axis. Every cell in the $5 \times 5$ grid is solved at 2023 ATB-Mid market conditions, PUE\,1.30, WACC 6.7\%, and zero carbon price. No cell reaches a non-negative heat-recovery premium. The most favourable corner (NOAK reactor at \$2{,}250/kW$_\mathrm{e}$ combined with a Bare-Low absorption chiller at \$450/kW$_\mathrm{c}$) lands at a premium of $-10\%$. The least favourable corner (FOAK reactor with Turnkey-High absorption) lands at $-503\%$. The premium $=0$ contour does not enter the rectangle defined by the published capital ranges. Capital cost reductions alone, even at the joint NOAK/Bare-Low limit of currently published estimates, are therefore insufficient to make colocated SMR with absorption cooling cheaper than grid supply at 2023 ERCOT prices and zero carbon cost.

\runinhead{Carbon-price crossover}
\label{sec:results:carbon}

After the market, technology-cost and financing envelope has been defined, the remaining main boundary is policy. Holding capital costs at the ATB-Mid baseline, the policy axis is the carbon price (Figure~\ref{fig:policy_sensitivity_summary}, panel a). For the grid-only Case~0 and the NGCC Case~3, the relationship is exactly linear because their net annual CO$_2$ footprints (306 and 385~ktCO$_2$/yr) are fixed by dispatch that does not respond to the carbon price. The two nuclear cases are only \emph{approximately} linear: because their objective rewards carbon-credited exports, a higher carbon price re-optimizes dispatch toward additional merchant export, so the net footprint itself grows more negative with price (Case~1 deepens from $-$407 to $-$451~ktCO$_2$/yr as the price rises from \$0 to \$100/tCO$_2$). Their carbon-cost curves consequently steepen slightly with price rather than holding a single slope, so the straight-line fits in Figure~\ref{fig:policy_sensitivity_summary}, panel a, are a close approximation over the plotted range rather than an exact identity.

The grid-only Case~0 trends upward at \$0.31~M/yr per \$/tCO$_2$ because the ERCOT grid mix is carbon-intensive. The two nuclear cases trend \emph{downward} (at an effective $-$\$0.43 to $-$\$0.45~M/yr per \$/tCO$_2$ across the plotted range) because their grid exports displace carbon-intensive grid generation and receive a corresponding carbon credit. Case~3 NGCC trends upward at \$0.39~M/yr per \$/tCO$_2$ because the on-site gas turbine carries the full direct emissions on the buyer side. As the carbon price rises, the two on-site options cross the grid-only baseline in opposite directions: NGCC, which starts below grid cost, loses its advantage and rises above the baseline near \$140/tCO$_2$, whereas the nuclear cases, which start well above it, fall below the baseline near \$188/tCO$_2$. Both crossings are linear extrapolations beyond the \$0--100/tCO$_2$ range solved in S6. Above \$188/tCO$_2$ the nuclear cases are the cheapest of the four, with Case~2 essentially indistinguishable from Case~1.

The cogeneration cooling provides no additional carbon-cost advantage; the absorption chiller competes with the equally low-carbon Case~1 export pathway rather than creating a separate credit. This crossover is a direct consequence of crediting the reactor's merchant exports at the ERCOT hourly average carbon intensity: the nuclear cases attain a net-negative annual footprint ($-429$~ktCO$_2$/yr for Case~2) only because the $\sim$1.3~TWh/yr of exported electricity displaces grid generation. The threshold is correspondingly sensitive to this accounting choice: halving the export credit roughly doubles it (to $\sim$\$357/tCO$_2$), and removing it entirely pushes it above \$2{,}000/tCO$_2$. Without an export-displacement credit, the nuclear cases do not beat grid procurement on carbon-cost grounds at any plausible price. Thus, Figure~\ref{fig:policy_sensitivity_summary} shifts the narrative from the technology-cost feasibility envelope in Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas} to the policy condition under which the nuclear cases collectively become competitive.

\subsection{Boundary synthesis and limitations}
\label{sec:results:synthesis}

Across the baseline run and eight sensitivity axes, four boundaries define when colocated SMR with absorption cooling approaches grid-supply competitiveness for a hyperscale data center in ERCOT.

The first is \emph{unit-size matching}. At the baseline 94.7~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ mean IT load, Case~2 exports 1.32~TWh/yr and has a $-222\%$ premium. Scaling the same load shape to 2.5$\times$ nearly balances exports and imports but leaves Case~2 at $-93\%$; scaling to 3.0$\times$ improves the premium to $-78\%$ while making the site a net importer. In annual dollars, however, the grid-cost gap stays near \$143--151~M/yr over the 1.0--3.0$\times$ range; the visible improvement is the decline in excess cost per MWh of IT load from \$172/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$ to \$61/MWh$_\mathrm{IT}$. Size matching therefore removes a major source of distortion but does not by itself open a viable region.

The second is a \emph{market-condition} threshold set by the ERCOT year regime and natural-gas price. At 2024 prices no on-site configuration is competitive; at 2023 prices only Case~3 NGCC is competitive; and at 2022 prices the gas case loses its advantage. This threshold determines the viable region for every on-site option.

The third is a \emph{capital-cost} frontier set by the joint SMR$\times$absorption CAPEX at the baseline load, 2023 market conditions, WACC 6.7\%, and zero carbon price. Inside the published cost rectangle, the joint frontier never crosses zero; the closest configuration to break-even is a NOAK reactor combined with a Bare-Low absorption chiller, which lands at $-10\%$. The one-dimensional reactor-CAPEX sweep indicates that NOAK capital cost is necessary, but the joint frontier shows that cheap absorption alone is insufficient and that the two favourable capital-cost conditions together still fall short.

The fourth is a \emph{policy} threshold set by the carbon price under the ATB-Mid capital assumptions. Without a price on carbon, no tested published capital-cost cell makes Case~2 competitive at the baseline load and WACC. Above \$188/tCO$_2$, both nuclear cases pass below the grid-only baseline and the Case~2 heat-recovery premium turns positive. The cogeneration cooling does not earn a separate carbon credit; the policy axis selects the nuclear cases collectively, not Case~2 specifically.

A central result is that the cogeneration cooling added in Case~2 does not by itself shift the competitiveness boundary relative to Case~1. In the baseline run Case~2 is 6.5 percentage points worse than Case~1; that gap narrows at high PUE and low absorption capital but never reverses the case ranking anywhere in the tested envelope. The absorption chiller adds about \$8.9~M/yr of annualized capital plus fixed O\&M and saves about \$5.5~M/yr of VCC electricity at the 2023 ERCOT LMP, leaving a small structural penalty for thermal integration at current prices. No single tested factor closes this gap: the penalty falls to \$1.5~M/yr at PUE\,1.50 but stays positive, and even the cheapest Bare-Low absorber leaves Case~2 below Case~1 at the NOAK corner. Reversing the ranking would require low absorption capital, cooling loads beyond hyperscale norms and grid prices rising faster than reactor amortization in combination, none of which occurs on its own within the ranges tested here.

\runinhead{Limitations}
\label{sec:results:limitations}

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. First, the size sweep scales a single hyperscale load shape uniformly; sites with markedly different temporal structure, such as mixed AI training and inference clusters, or different climates would shift the absorption value proposition. Second, the carbon-price analysis assigns the same uniform price to the hourly average grid carbon intensity for credit and to the on-site direct emissions for debit, which is a strong assumption about how a future carbon policy would be implemented; the hourly average emission factor used here is itself a proxy for the true marginal emission factor, and a merit-order marginal series would refine the avoided-emissions credit. Third, demand charges and ancillary-service revenues are not modelled. These omissions do not all act in the same direction: the load-shape and marginal-emission assumptions could move the numerical boundary either way, but leaving out transmission, ancillary and demand charges is one-sided, because restoring the full delivered grid cost would raise the Case~0 baseline and narrow every on-site premium, so the penalties reported here are conservative against the on-site configurations. A separate caveat concerns reliability: Case~3 is modelled as a single islanded NGCC with no redundant unit, grid tie or outage cost, whereas Cases~1 and 2 retain grid import to cover outages; meeting the five-nines availability assumed in Section~\ref{sec:intro} would raise the Case~3 cost and narrow its 17\% advantage. Two final caveats follow from the analysis above: the reported premium reflects the smallest deployable reactor rather than an optimized multi-module buildout, and the carbon-price crossover depends on crediting merchant exports at the grid-average intensity. All of these should be evaluated with region-specific tariff, market-service and marginal-emissions data before procurement.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% WRITE-SECTION-TARGET: conclusion

A linear-programming co-optimization of 100 annual hourly scenario runs spanning the four-case baseline, data-center size, three ERCOT years, three SMR capital cost trajectories, a $5\times5$ joint reactor/absorption capital cost grid, three carbon prices, and three WACC levels indicates that colocated SMR with double-effect absorption cooling is a conditional rather than a categorical cost-competitive solution. At 2023 ATB-Mid prices for the baseline 200~MW campus, the cogeneration configuration is 222\% more expensive than grid supply (a heat-recovery premium of $-222\%$) and approximately \$4.2~M/yr more expensive than an SMR-only plant; absorption-chiller capital outweighs the cooling-electricity savings it produces. Increasing the data-center load to 2.5$\times$ the baseline nearly eliminates the BWRX-300 merchant-export surplus, but Case~2 still lands at $-93\%$, showing that unit-size matching is important but insufficient. At the baseline load, WACC and zero carbon price, no cell of the published joint reactor / absorption-chiller capital cost rectangle contains a configuration that beats grid supply; the most favourable corner (NOAK reactor plus a \$450/kW$_\mathrm{c}$ absorption chiller) lands at $-10\%$. Under the separate ATB-Mid carbon-price sweep, a carbon price near \$188/tCO$_2$ closes the gap to grid supply for both SMR configurations; this threshold is extrapolated beyond the simulated range and hinges on crediting merchant exports at the grid-average carbon intensity, exceeding \$2{,}000/tCO$_2$ without that credit. The cogeneration option does not earn a separable carbon credit above the SMR-only option. For siting and procurement decisions in ERCOT, colocated SMR with absorption cooling should therefore be treated as a strategy whose competitiveness depends on several necessary conditions, each tested here in isolation: enough load to absorb the smallest commercial reactor; an ERCOT price regime in which on-site supply can compete with grid procurement; reactor and absorption-chiller capital costs jointly at the NOAK/Bare-Low corner of the published rectangle, which alone still leaves a 10\% gap; and a non-trivial carbon value, with favourable financing able to compress but not close the residual gap. The scenario envelope and input provenance documented in the Supplementary Information provide the basis for extending the boundary analysis to other ISO regions, climates, and data-center sizes.
