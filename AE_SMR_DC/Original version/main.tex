\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

U.S. data centers are reshaping electricity demand. Consumption at U.S. data centers nearly doubled between 2019 and 2023 and is projected by the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory to reach between 6.7\% and 12\% of total U.S. electricity by 2028 \citep{shehabi2024}. The International Energy Agency projects global data center consumption to roughly double to 945~TWh by 2030, with the United States and China accounting for nearly 80\% of that growth \citep{iea2024electricity,iea2025energyAi}. The Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which serves a substantial share of the new U.S. data-center pipeline, expects its large-load interconnection queue to exceed 138~GW by 2030, an increase that requires firm, dispatchable capacity beyond what variable renewables can supply alone \citep{ercot2025longterm}. The latest U.S. Annual Energy Outlook places much of this growth in regions managed by ERCOT and PJM, where transmission queues and capacity adequacy already constrain interconnection timelines \citep{eia2026aeo}.

Data-center loads place an unusual set of demands on the supplying generator. The workload is highly continuous, with capacity factors close to one, and modern hyperscale facilities require power availability at five nines or better. Cooling represents the dominant non-IT energy use, typically 20\% to 40\% of total facility consumption, and rises sharply with ambient wet-bulb temperature in inland sites such as Texas. These characteristics make data centers a natural counterpart to firm, low-carbon generators that match base-load operating patterns. Small modular reactors (SMRs), in particular, have re-entered commercial conversation because they offer dispatchable carbon-free output at unit sizes between roughly 50~MWe and 300~MWe, comparable to a single hyperscale campus \citep{doe2024liftoff,atb2024nuclear,asuega2023}.

Two streams of prior work converge on the system studied here. The first examines whether SMRs can supply data-center electricity at a cost competitive with grid supply. Asuega et al.~\citep{asuega2023} performed a Monte Carlo techno-economic analysis of light-water, gas-cooled, and molten-salt SMR configurations against natural gas combined cycle and conventional large reactors, finding that a 12~$\times$~77~MWe light-water SMR plant exhibits a lower mean overnight capital cost and shorter construction timeline than a single 1~GW reactor but remains above grid prices in most market regions absent a carbon constraint. The Idaho National Laboratory meta-analysis of 48 advanced-reactor cost data sets reaches a similar conclusion, with capital cost estimates dispersed widely between first-of-a-kind and nth-of-a-kind cases \citep{inl2024metaanalysis}. The U.S. Department of Energy Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Advanced Nuclear report consolidates this view and highlights long-term power purchase agreements with hyperscale technology firms as a financing mechanism that may lower effective capital cost over time through learning-by-doing \citep{doe2024liftoff}. Several 2024 and 2025 industry agreements between hyperscalers and reactor developers, including the Microsoft-Constellation Three Mile Island restart and the Google-Kairos Power 500~MW deal, reflect a shift from policy-driven to commercially-driven nuclear deployment for data-center supply \citep{eia2024nuclearDC}. The 2024 NREL Annual Technology Baseline distinguishes 300~MWe SMR capital cost between first-of-a-kind, mid, and nth-of-a-kind trajectories, with the gap between the two extremes exceeding a factor of six in 2024 USD \citep{atb2024nuclear}.

The second stream studies cooling-side thermal integration. Cooling consumes 20\% to 40\% of data-center energy in air-cooled facilities, and single-effect lithium-bromide water (LiBr-H$_2$O) absorption chillers convert low-grade heat into chilled water at coefficients of performance (COP) near 0.7 \citep{amiri2021}. Amiri et al.~\citep{amiri2021} reported annual energy savings of 4.3~GWh to 13.0~GWh for a 4.5~MW to 13.5~MW data center retrofitted with an absorption chiller driven by IT-side waste heat, with corresponding emissions savings of 3{,}068~tCO$_2$ to 9{,}208~tCO$_2$ per year. Cui et al.~\citep{cui2025switchable} proposed a switchable multistage absorption design targeting ultra-low-grade waste heat below conventional driving-temperature requirements. Double-effect designs reach COP between 1.1 and 1.4 but require higher driving temperatures, typically above 130~$^\circ$C, that are more easily obtained from a steam-cycle reactor than from server-rack exhaust. Across both streams, the absorption submodel is sensitive to ambient wet-bulb temperature: a higher cooling-water inlet temperature drives the LiBr solution toward the crystallization line, forcing a switch to vapor-compression backup \citep{cui2025switchable}. In Texas, this de-rating concentrates in July and August afternoons, when wet-bulb temperatures regularly exceed 26~$^\circ$C.

Three gaps limit the use of existing results for siting and procurement decisions in markets such as ERCOT. First, prior studies treat waste-heat utilization through absorption cooling as a categorical benefit, comparing a no-recovery baseline against a fully integrated configuration without identifying the technology- and market-specific conditions under which the integration is economically net-positive. Second, hourly cooling physics, including wet-bulb-driven COP de-rating and crystallization downtime, has not been combined with multi-year market data covering the recent range of Henry Hub gas prices, ERCOT locational marginal prices, and reactor capital cost trajectories. Third, sensitivity analyses in this space are typically one-dimensional, reporting how outcomes change as a single parameter varies, and do not deliver a multi-axis envelope that practitioners can use to map a candidate site or financing structure onto a competitive or non-competitive region. Without such an envelope, claims that SMR with absorption cooling is or is not viable rest on the choice of the baseline year and the reactor capital cost path.

This paper presents a techno-economic boundary analysis of colocated SMR with absorption cooling for a 200~MW hyperscale data center in ERCOT. A mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) framework co-optimizes annualized total cost across power generation, cooling supply, and battery storage at hourly resolution for full 8760-hour years using year-resolved ERCOT day-ahead prices, Henry Hub gas prices, and Houston wet-bulb temperatures from 2022 to 2024. Four configurations are compared: grid-only supply with electric chillers (Case~0), an SMR plant with electric chillers (Case~1), an SMR plant coupled to a double-effect LiBr-H$_2$O absorption chiller with vapor-compression backup and a crystallization gate (Case~3), and an on-site natural gas combined cycle plant with electric chillers (Case~4). A multi-axis sensitivity design exposes each configuration to ranges in power usage effectiveness, market year, battery deployment, reactor capital cost trajectory, absorption chiller capital cost, heat opportunity cost from steam extraction, and grid electricity or carbon price.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, the analysis quantifies the cost-competitiveness envelope of SMR with absorption cooling jointly over reactor capital cost, absorption chiller capital cost, and heat opportunity cost rather than along a single axis. Second, it shows how the dominant axis of economic competitiveness shifts among market year, reactor capital cost path, and thermal integration parameters, identifying which conditions must be satisfied jointly rather than independently. Third, it provides a reproducible MILP framework, with all input data and run artifacts archived, that practitioners can re-parameterize for other ISO regions or data-center sizes.

Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results and discusses the boundary conditions for thermal integration and the implications for site selection and procurement. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
