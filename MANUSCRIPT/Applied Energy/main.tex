\begin{abstract}
% Single-paragraph informative abstract (200--300 words) on the corrected model.
% Elsevier permits AI-assisted writing with disclosure; the content has been
% reviewed and edited by the authors, who take full responsibility for it.
Data centers are becoming large, continuous electricity loads, and their rapid growth is intensifying demand for firm, low-carbon supply. Colocating them with small modular reactors (SMRs) that also recover heat for cooling has been proposed to meet that demand, yet the economics remain uncertain under current market and policy conditions. Here we develop an hourly techno-economic co-optimization of reactor operation, cascaded steam extraction, double-effect absorption cooling and bidirectional grid exchange, and use it to evaluate a 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ hyperscale data center in the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) region. A mixed-integer linear program co-optimizes dispatch at 8760-hour resolution across 100 model runs spanning market year, cooling efficiency, reactor and absorption-chiller capital cost, carbon price, financing and campus size, with the Section~45Y clean-electricity production tax credit applied to the nuclear cases. At 2023 mid-range reactor capital, the SMR configurations cost 49--62\% more than grid supply even with the credit; the viable region opens near \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ of reactor capital, and nth-of-a-kind capital makes the SMR cases 77--89\% cheaper than the grid with no carbon price. At mid-range capital, carbon prices of \$53--64~tCO$_2^{-1}$ close the remaining gap within the solved range. Absorption cooling dispatches against the electricity price, supplying 38\% of annual cooling at the baseline, but raises annualized cost by \$9.2~M~yr$^{-1}$. Reactor capital cost, not heat recovery, governs whether colocated nuclear cogeneration is competitive, yielding a transferable boundary map for site-, climate- and policy-specific procurement decisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Background and motivation}
\label{sec:intro:background}

Data centers are emerging as a major driver of electricity-demand growth after a decade in which efficiency gains moderated their global energy use \citep{masanet2020datacenter}. U.S. data-center electricity consumption nearly doubled between 2019 and 2023 and is projected to reach 6.7--12\% of total U.S. electricity by 2028 \citep{shehabi2024}; globally, electricity use from data centers is projected to roughly double to 945~TWh by 2030, with the United States and China accounting for nearly 80\% of the increase \citep{iea2024electricity,iea2025energyAi}. In the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which serves a substantial share of the new U.S. data-center pipeline, large-load interconnection requests already exceed the scale of conventional planning margins, and the 2026 Annual Energy Outlook places much of this growth in ERCOT- and PJM-managed regions where transmission queues and capacity adequacy already constrain interconnection timelines \citep{ercot2025longterm,eia2026aeo}.

This growth poses a supply problem that is not only about annual energy. Hyperscale campuses are highly continuous loads, with capacity factors close to one, and modern facilities require power availability at five nines or higher. Cooling is the dominant non-information-technology (IT) energy use, typically 20--40\% of facility consumption, and it rises sharply during the hot and humid hours that characterize inland sites such as Texas \citep{greenGrid2012pue,ashrae2021thermal}. These characteristics make data centers a natural counterpart to firm, low-carbon generators with baseload operating patterns. Small modular reactors (SMRs), in particular, have re-entered procurement and planning discussions because they provide dispatchable carbon-free output at unit sizes between roughly 50 and 300~MW$_\mathrm{e}$, comparable to a single hyperscale campus \citep{mignacca2020smrFinance,doe2024liftoff,atb2024nuclear,asuega2023}.

\subsection{Prior work on SMR economics and cooling-side thermal integration}
\label{sec:intro:priorwork}

Two streams of prior work converge on the system studied here. The first asks whether SMRs can supply data-center electricity at a cost competitive with grid procurement. Asuega et al.~\citep{asuega2023} performed a Monte Carlo techno-economic analysis of light-water, gas-cooled and molten-salt SMR configurations against natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) and conventional gigawatt-scale reactors, finding that a multi-unit light-water SMR plant has a lower mean overnight capital cost and a shorter construction timeline than a single large reactor but remains above grid prices in most market regions without a carbon constraint. The Idaho National Laboratory meta-analysis of advanced-reactor cost data sets reaches a similar conclusion, with capital-cost estimates dispersed widely between first-of-a-kind (FOAK) and nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) cases \citep{inl2024metaanalysis}. Broader SMR-finance reviews and nuclear construction-cost studies trace this dispersion to learning, standardization, project delivery and financing structure \citep{mignacca2020smrFinance,nea2020constructionCost}, and the Pathways to Commercial Liftoff assessment identifies long-term offtake agreements with hyperscale technology firms as a mechanism that may lower effective capital cost over time \citep{doe2024liftoff}. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Annual Technology Baseline distinguishes 300~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ SMR capital cost among FOAK, mid-range and NOAK trajectories, with the gap between the extremes exceeding a factor of six \citep{atb2024nuclear}. Current U.S. policy adds a second axis of uncertainty: the technology-neutral Section~45Y clean-electricity production tax credit (PTC), for which a new-build zero-emission reactor qualifies, can materially change the operating economics of the plant, but only when the pre-credit gap to grid supply is small enough for the credit to close it.

The second stream studies cooling-side thermal integration. Reviews of data-center cooling technology identify low-grade waste heat as an underused resource but emphasize that recovery value depends on sink temperature, operating hours and auxiliary power \citep{ebrahimi2014review,yuan2023wasteHeatReview}. Single-effect lithium-bromide--water (LiBr--H$_2$O) absorption chillers convert low-grade heat into chilled water at coefficients of performance (COP) near 0.7; Amiri et al.~\citep{amiri2021} reported annual energy savings of several gigawatt-hours for a data center retrofitted with an absorption chiller driven by IT-side waste heat. Cui et al.~\citep{cui2025switchable} proposed a switchable multistage absorption design targeting ultra-low-grade heat below conventional driving-temperature requirements. Double-effect machines reach COP between 1.1 and 1.4 but require higher driving temperatures, typically above 130~$^\circ$C, that a steam-cycle reactor can supply more readily than server-rack exhaust \citep{srikhirin2001absorption,herold2016absorption}. Across both streams, the absorption submodel is sensitive to ambient wet-bulb temperature: a higher cooling-water inlet temperature drives the LiBr solution toward the crystallization line, forcing a switch to vapor-compression backup \citep{cui2025switchable,herold2016absorption}. In Texas, this derating concentrates in July and August afternoons. Recent nuclear--data-center assessments extend the cost question to mixed SMR and distributed-energy portfolios, demand-growth equilibrium and integrated power--thermal architectures \citep{debnath2025smrDER,you2025dynamicEquilibrium,bhowmik2026nuclearDC,zhang2026nuclearDC}, but they rarely combine hourly cooling physics, multi-year electricity and gas prices, reactor capital-cost trajectories, production-tax-credit policy and carbon accounting within a single co-optimization.

\subsection{Contributions and paper organization}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

Together these streams leave the siting and procurement decision underdetermined. Studies of thermally integrated absorption cooling have largely treated heat reuse as a categorical benefit, comparing a no-recovery baseline against a fully integrated configuration without identifying the technology- and market-specific conditions under which integration actually pays. Hourly cooling physics, including wet-bulb-driven COP derating and crystallization downtime, has rarely been combined with multi-year market data spanning the recent range of Henry Hub gas prices and ERCOT locational marginal prices (LMPs), reactor capital-cost trajectories and current tax-credit policy. And because sensitivity analyses in this setting are usually one-dimensional, they provide no multi-axis envelope that maps a candidate site or financing structure onto a competitive or non-competitive region. The needed result is not a single levelized cost but a boundary map: when does a colocated reactor beat grid procurement, when does absorption cooling actually add value, and which assumptions move the boundary?

Here we present a techno-economic boundary analysis of a 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ hyperscale data center colocated with a BWRX-300 SMR and a double-effect absorption chiller driven by cascaded high-pressure to low-pressure turbine extraction. A mixed-integer linear program co-optimizes power supply, cooling and grid exchange at hourly dispatch resolution over 8760-hour annual horizons, using 2022--2024 ERCOT price, IT-load and Houston wet-bulb traces, with daily Henry Hub spot prices plus basis applied to hourly gas-dispatch intervals and optional battery storage evaluated as a sensitivity. We compare grid supply, reactor supply without heat recovery, reactor supply with absorption cooling, and an on-site NGCC across 100 model runs spanning data-center scale, cooling efficiency, market year, battery deployment, reactor and absorption capital cost, carbon price and weighted-average cost of capital (WACC), with the Section~45Y PTC applied to the nuclear cases.

The analysis makes three advances. First, it couples an hourly absorption submodel that resolves wet-bulb-driven COP derating and a LiBr crystallization gate to multi-year ERCOT price, Henry Hub and reactor-capital trajectories within one co-optimization under current Section~45Y policy. Second, it maps the cost-competitiveness envelope of SMR with absorption cooling jointly over reactor and absorption-chiller capital cost, separating this two-axis capital-cost frontier from one-axis movements toward parity along the policy, financing, market-year and unit-size axes. Third, it converts the resulting envelope into procurement-relevant boundary conditions that generalize beyond ERCOT to other markets, climates and data-center sizes. We find that nuclear cogeneration is a conditional strategy: at mid-range reactor capital it remains well above grid cost, but the viable region opens near \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ of reactor capital with no carbon price, while absorption cooling dispatches against the electricity price and does not set the economic boundary.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the system configurations, the optimization formulation, the absorption submodel, the data inputs and the scenario design. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the baseline economics and the boundary-condition sensitivities, and discusses the implications for site selection and procurement. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Scenario definitions, secondary endpoints and input provenance are documented in the Supplementary Information.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{System configurations}
\label{sec:methods:system}

The analysis compares four configurations that supply electricity and chilled water to a single hyperscale data center in Houston, Texas, rated at 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$. Each configuration uses the same data-center load profile, vapor-compression chiller technology when required, and financial parameters; the configurations differ only in the electricity supply pathway and in whether the cooling chain uses recovered reactor heat. Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} illustrates the cogeneration mechanism and the grid and storage interfaces for the fully integrated thermal-integration case. Cases~0, 1 and 3 are reduced versions of this layout in which selected blocks are disabled. Table~\ref{tab:cases} summarizes the four configurations.

Case~0 represents the conventional grid-supplied data center. All electricity is purchased from ERCOT at the day-ahead LMP, and all cooling is produced by a water-cooled centrifugal vapor-compression chiller (VCC) with a design-point COP of 3.2 at the Houston design wet-bulb, relieved hourly by the condenser-water benefit of cooler hours. This case is the benchmark for the grid-cost margin reported throughout the paper. Case~1 replaces grid imports with on-site BWRX-300 generation while retaining the same VCC; heat at the turbine condenser is rejected to the cooling tower and is not recovered. Bidirectional grid exchange is retained so that excess electricity can be sold to ERCOT and imports can cover demand during the scheduled refueling outage. Case~2 adds a 160~MW$_\mathrm{c}$ nominal double-effect LiBr--H$_2$O absorption unit fed by a mid-pressure extraction tap at the high-pressure to low-pressure (LP) crossover; effective delivered cooling is limited by the generator's steam-side capacity, the wet-bulb-derated COP, the installed nameplate and the crystallization availability gate. The cascade keeps the LP turbine in service, so diverted steam incurs the lost LP expansion work net of the hot-condensate-return credit rather than the full turbine efficiency penalty. A 160~MW$_\mathrm{c}$ VCC remains installed at the same capacity as in the VCC-only cases: it carries the full cooling load during the refueling outage and whenever a low electricity price makes steam diversion uneconomic, so the two chillers share dispatch rather than splitting into primary and emergency roles. Case~3 replaces the SMR with a 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ on-site F-class NGCC, retains the VCC and disconnects from the grid to isolate the value of replacing grid procurement with a fossil-fueled on-site alternative.

The data-center IT load follows an annual hourly trace synthesized from a colocation profile measured in calendar 2018 and scaled to a 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ facility, with a peak hourly load of 142.4~MW$_\mathrm{e}$, an annual average of 94.7~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ and a mean-to-peak utilization of 0.67. Because the trace's weekly rhythm belongs to its measurement year, it is circularly shifted by whole days so that day-of-week and hour-of-day align with each market year's calendar before being joined to the price, weather and carbon series. The same trace is used in every case and every scenario; the IT load is not a decision variable. \begin{equation}
\label{eq:cool_demand}
Q_\mathrm{cool}^\mathrm{dem}(t) \;=\; \frac{P_\mathrm{IT}(t)}{\eta_\mathrm{ch}},
\end{equation}
where $\eta_\mathrm{ch}=0.90$ accounts for the chilled-water distribution chain. Each scenario is labeled by its full-load vapor-compression PUE, $1+1/(\eta_\mathrm{ch}\,\mathrm{COP}_\mathrm{v})$, which is 1.35 at the baseline design-point COP of 3.2. The dispatch-realized annual PUE, $1+P_\mathrm{cool}/P_\mathrm{IT}$, is lower for the vapor-compression cases, near 1.31, because the part-load COP hump and the wet-bulb relief both run the chiller more efficiently than at its full-load design point. It falls to about 1.19 in Case~2, where steam-driven absorption removes the electric cooling load of the high-priced hours. The cooling-efficiency sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:results:pue}) sweeps the chiller COP so that this full-load PUE label spans $\{1.10, 1.30, 1.50\}$, the range between high-efficiency and legacy hyperscale practice \citep{greenGrid2012pue,ashrae2021thermal}.

\subsection{Optimization formulation}
\label{sec:methods:opt}

The four cases are formulated as separate dispatch problems on the same 8760-hour horizon. Cases~0 and 3 reduce to closed-form annual cost evaluations because neither leaves a dispatch choice open: Case~0 meets every hour's load with grid purchases at the prevailing price, and the islanded Case~3 must follow load with its single on-site generator, so each hour's cost follows directly from the realized load. Cases~1 and 2, whose hours are coupled by reactor ramping and the import--export trade decision, are mixed-integer linear programs with two sources of integer structure: the special-ordered-set (SOS2) representation of the VCC part-load curve described below, which is non-convex and cannot be relaxed, and an hourly binary interlock between grid imports and exports, which removes the degenerate simultaneous-trade solutions that are cost-neutral at a single hourly price and would otherwise make reported gross trade volumes solver-arbitrary. No unit-commitment binaries are introduced, and round-trip losses in the battery block already make simultaneous charge and discharge unprofitable. The mixed-integer programs of Cases~1 and 2 are formulated in Pyomo 6.9 and solved with Gurobi 13.0, using the barrier method for the root relaxation, a relative optimality-gap tolerance of $10^{-4}$ and a fixed random seed; all 70 such runs in the 100-run ledger solve to this tolerance, and the 30 Case~0 and Case~3 runs are closed-form evaluations that involve no solver. The same accounting structure is applied to all four cases so that cross-case comparisons remain on a common basis.

\runinhead{Reactor and turbine block (Cases~1 and 2)}
The BWRX-300 reactor is modeled as a continuous thermal source with bounded ramping and a scheduled refueling outage that realizes the fleet-average capacity factor. The reactor block enforces
\begin{align}
\underline{P}_\mathrm{rx} \;\le\; & P_\mathrm{rx}(t) \;\le\; \overline{P}_\mathrm{rx}, &\forall\, t \in \mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{T}_\mathrm{out}, \label{eq:rx_bounds}\\
P_\mathrm{rx}(t) \;=\; & 0, &\forall\, t \in \mathcal{T}_\mathrm{out}, \label{eq:rx_outage}\\
\big|P_\mathrm{rx}(t) - P_\mathrm{rx}(t-1)\big| \;\le\; & \Delta P_\mathrm{rx}^{\max}, &\forall\, t \ge 1 \text{ outside } \mathcal{T}_\mathrm{out}, \label{eq:rx_ramp}
\end{align}
with $\overline{P}_\mathrm{rx}=870~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{th}$, $\underline{P}_\mathrm{rx}=435~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{th}$ (50\% minimum-load fraction from the BWRX-300 design basis \citep{gehitachi2024bwrx300}), and $\Delta P_\mathrm{rx}^{\max}=522~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{th}~\mathrm{h}^{-1}$ corresponding to a 1\,\%~min$^{-1}$ ramp. The outage window $\mathcal{T}_\mathrm{out}$ comprises $\mathrm{round}\!\big((1-\overline{\mathrm{CF}})\cdot 8760\big) = 701$ contiguous hours beginning 15~March (hour 1752 of the trace; 14~March in the leap-year 2024 record), in the mild-load shoulder season when U.S. plants schedule refueling, so a plant at full power in every online hour realizes $\overline{\mathrm{CF}}=0.92$ to within 0.003\%, the mature U.S.\ light-water-reactor fleet average \citep{eia2025capacityfactors}. During the outage the data center buys grid power at the prevailing price, so the cost of backup supply is priced rather than assumed away; shutdown and startup at the window boundaries are managed procedures excluded from the normal load-following ramp limits.

The Rankine steam cycle is represented by a cascaded high-pressure to low-pressure turbine whose gross output follows a Willans line in reactor thermal input, fitted by least squares to the turbine part-load heat-rate characteristic and constrained through the design point (870~MW$_\mathrm{th}$ $\rightarrow$ 300~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ gross, $R^2>0.999$); the no-load intercept makes the part-load efficiency droop explicit -- a constant-efficiency model would overstate gross output at minimum load by about 8\%. With slope $a_\mathrm{w}=0.371~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{e}~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{th}^{-1}$, intercept $b_\mathrm{w}=22.9~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{e}$ and extraction penalty $\alpha_\mathrm{w}=0.20~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{e}~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{th}^{-1}$ for mid-pressure steam diverted to the absorber,
\begin{align}
P_\mathrm{tg}(t) \;=\; & a_\mathrm{w}\, P_\mathrm{rx}(t) \;-\; b_\mathrm{w} \;-\; \alpha_\mathrm{w}\, Q_\mathrm{ext}(t), \label{eq:turb_willans}\\
P_\mathrm{tn}(t) \;=\; & (1 - \xi_\mathrm{aux})\, P_\mathrm{tg}(t), \label{eq:turb_aux}
\end{align}
with $\xi_\mathrm{aux}=0.10$ and $P_\mathrm{tg}(t)=0$ during the outage window. The extraction coefficient follows from a steam-table heat balance of the 7~bar / 165~$^\circ$C crossover tap: each kilogram of extracted steam forfeits about 480~kJ of low-pressure expansion work, delivers about 1{,}730~kJ to the absorber generator, and returns about 145~kJ of equivalent work through the hot-condensate credit to the feedwater train, giving $\alpha_\mathrm{w} \approx (480-145)/1730 \approx 0.19$, rounded to 0.20. Because diverted steam still expands through the high-pressure turbine before extraction, this is roughly half the cost of charging the full turbine efficiency against extracted heat, as would be appropriate for a parallel main-steam tap; the Supplementary Information sweeps $\alpha_\mathrm{w}$ over 0.08--0.25.

\runinhead{Absorption-chiller submodel (Case~2)}
The double-effect LiBr--H$_2$O absorption chiller is described by a time-varying COP and a binary availability indicator that captures LiBr crystallization risk \citep{srikhirin2001absorption,herold2016absorption}. With $T_\mathrm{wb,0}=26\,^\circ$C as the Houston design wet-bulb, $\mathrm{COP}_\mathrm{a}^{\,0}=1.10$ as the COP at the design wet-bulb, $\delta=0.015\,^\circ$C$^{-1}$ as the linear derating slope, and $\overline{\mathrm{COP}}_\mathrm{a}=1.30$ as the nameplate cap, the chiller COP at wet-bulb $T_\mathrm{wb}(t)$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cop_abs}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{COP}_\mathrm{a}\!\big(T_\mathrm{wb}(t)\big) =
\max\Big\{&
  \min\big[\mathrm{COP}_\mathrm{a}^{\,0}
  - \delta\,(T_\mathrm{wb}(t)-T_\mathrm{wb,0}), \\
& \overline{\mathrm{COP}}_\mathrm{a}\big],\; 0.5\Big\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The lower bound of 0.5 is a numerical safeguard; Houston wet-bulbs in the weather record do not reduce a physical chiller to this value because the crystallization gate engages first. With $\Delta T_\mathrm{cw}=5$~K as the cooling-tower approach and $T_\mathrm{cry}=34\,^\circ$C as the LiBr shutdown threshold on the cooling-water inlet -- the 31~$^\circ$C Houston design point plus the datasheet's 3~K crystallization safety margin, inside the 34--35~$^\circ$C vendor envelope for double-effect machines -- the availability indicator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abs_avail}
a(t) \;=\; \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } T_\mathrm{wb}(t) + \Delta T_\mathrm{cw} \le T_\mathrm{cry} \text{ and } t \notin \mathcal{T}_\mathrm{out}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}
\end{equation}
so the absorption chiller is forced fully offline whenever the cooling-water inlet would exceed the crystallization threshold and throughout the reactor refueling outage, inside which its own annual maintenance is scheduled (it has no steam source then, so no separate continuous availability derate is applied). With the $\Delta T_\mathrm{cw}=5$~K tower approach the crystallization condition is a wet-bulb gate at $T_\mathrm{cry}-\Delta T_\mathrm{cw}=34-5=29\,^\circ$C, the value marked in Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}a; the 2023 record peaks at 28~$^\circ$C, so the gate never engages in the baseline year. Delivered absorption cooling is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abs_yield}
Q_\mathrm{a}(t) \;=\; \mathrm{COP}_\mathrm{a}\!\big(T_\mathrm{wb}(t)\big)\cdot a(t)\cdot Q_\mathrm{ext}(t),
\end{equation}
subject to two explicit physical limits: the generator's steam-side capacity, sized to deliver nameplate cooling at the design-point COP ($Q_\mathrm{ext}(t) \le 160/1.10 \approx 145~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{th}$), and the installed cooling nameplate ($Q_\mathrm{a}(t) \le 160~\mathrm{MW}_\mathrm{c}$), which binds in cool hours when the COP rises above its design value. A parasitic electric load $\beta_\mathrm{a}\, Q_\mathrm{a}(t)$ with $\beta_\mathrm{a}=0.035~\mathrm{kW}_\mathrm{e}~\mathrm{kW}_\mathrm{c}^{-1}$ powers the solution and refrigerant pumps (0.020) and the cooling-tower fans (0.015). The VCC carries the residual cooling load.

\runinhead{Cooling, BESS and grid blocks}
Vapor-compression cooling links its electric input to its cooling output through a part-load COP. A water-cooled centrifugal chiller gains efficiency at reduced load, with the COP peaking near 40--50\% of rated duty before declining at very low load, following the integrated part-load value of ANSI/AHRI Standard~550/590 \citep{ahri2020standard550}. The chiller electricity is therefore a non-convex, piecewise-linear function of the cooling delivered, $P_\mathrm{v}(t)=f_\mathrm{v}\!\big(Q_\mathrm{v}(t)\big)$, anchored at the design-point value $\mathrm{COP}_\mathrm{v}=3.2$ at the 26~$^\circ$C Houston design wet-bulb. To keep the comparison between the two cooling technologies even-handed, the VCC carries the same relative wet-bulb response as the absorption chiller's COP model: the design-point COP is multiplied each hour by $\min\!\big[1+0.0136\,(26-T_\mathrm{wb}(t)),\,1.18\big]$, the absorber's relief slope (0.015/1.10 per kelvin) and cap (1.30/1.10) expressed relative to its design point, so cooler condenser water benefits both chillers identically and warmer hours derate both. In the closed-form Cases~0 and 3 the chiller is evaluated directly at the realized load fraction; in the nuclear Cases~1 and 2 the same curve enters the optimization as an SOS2 piecewise map. The lithium-ion BESS, sized at 100~MWh and 50~MW (C/2), follows the standard state-of-charge dynamics
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bess_soc}
E_\mathrm{B}(t) \;=\; (1-\rho_\mathrm{B})\,E_\mathrm{B}(t-1) + \big(\sqrt{\eta_\mathrm{rt}}\,B^{+}(t) - B^{-}(t)/\sqrt{\eta_\mathrm{rt}}\big)\Delta t,
\end{equation}
with initial state of charge fixed at $E_\mathrm{B}(0)=0.5\,\mathrm{Cap}_\mathrm{E}$ and cyclically restored through $E_\mathrm{B}(|\mathcal{T}|) = E_\mathrm{B}(0)$, $\eta_\mathrm{rt}=0.85$, $\rho_\mathrm{B}=4.17\times10^{-5}$\,h$^{-1}$, and state-of-charge bounds $[0.05,\, 0.95]\,\mathrm{Cap}_\mathrm{E}$. Grid exchange is bounded by the point of common coupling (PCC) and interlocked by an hourly binary $y(t)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grid_interlock}
0 \le P_\mathrm{g}^{+}(t) \le \overline{P}_\mathrm{pcc}\, y(t), \qquad
0 \le P_\mathrm{g}^{-}(t) \le \overline{P}_\mathrm{pcc}\, \big(1 - y(t)\big),
\end{equation}
so the site never buys and sells in the same hour; without the interlock, simultaneous trade is cost-neutral at a single hourly price and gross trade volumes would be degenerate. $\overline{P}_\mathrm{pcc}=300$~MW for the baseline campus; its treatment in the size-matching sweep is described under scenario design.

\runinhead{Energy balances}
Two equality constraints hold every hour. The electric balance equates supply, BESS discharge and grid imports to the IT load, chiller drives, absorber parasitics, BESS charge and grid exports:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:elec_balance}
P_\mathrm{tn}(t) + P_\mathrm{g}^{+}(t) + B^{-}(t)
\;=\; & P_\mathrm{IT}(t) + P_\mathrm{v}(t) + \beta_\mathrm{a} Q_\mathrm{a}(t) \notag\\
\;+\; & P_\mathrm{g}^{-}(t) + B^{+}(t).
\end{align}
The cooling balance equates total cooling supplied to the cooling demand of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cool_demand}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cool_balance}
Q_\mathrm{a}(t) + Q_\mathrm{v}(t) \;=\; Q_\mathrm{cool}^\mathrm{dem}(t).
\end{equation}
For Cases~0, 1 and 3 the absorption variables $Q_\mathrm{a}(t)$, $Q_\mathrm{ext}(t)$ are fixed to zero; for Case~3 the reactor and turbine variables are removed and the IT and chiller load is served entirely by the NGCC block at a full-load higher-heating-value (HHV) efficiency $\eta_\mathrm{hhv}=0.495$, with the part-load heat-rate penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ngcc_fuel_cost}.

\runinhead{Objective}
The optimization minimizes total annualized cost (TAC) summed over seven accounting buckets:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tac}
\begin{aligned}
\min\; \mathrm{TAC} ={}&
\sum_k \mathrm{CRF}_k\,\mathrm{CAPEX}_k S_k + \mathrm{FOM} + \mathrm{VOM}\\
& + \Phi_\mathrm{fuel} + \Phi_\mathrm{grid} + \Phi_\mathrm{CO_2} - \Phi_\mathrm{ptc},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the first term annualizes overnight CAPEX across reactor, turbine, absorption and VCC chillers, BESS and NGCC sized at $S_k$, each amortized over its own engineering life $N_k$ through an asset-specific capital recovery factor (CRF; reactor island 40~yr per the NLR ATB SMR convention, chillers 25~yr, NGCC 30~yr, battery 15~yr); $\mathrm{FOM}=\sum_k \mathrm{FOM}_k S_k$ aggregates fixed operations and maintenance, and $v_x$ denotes the variable-O\&M coefficient of stream $x$ (Table~\ref{tab:equipment}); the variable terms are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{VOM} ={}&
  \sum_t \big[v_\mathrm{rx} P_\mathrm{tn}(t) + v_\mathrm{v} Q_\mathrm{v}(t) + v_\mathrm{a} Q_\mathrm{a}(t)\big]\Delta t
  \notag\\
& + \sum_t v_\mathrm{B}\big(B^{+}(t)+B^{-}(t)\big)\Delta t, \label{eq:vom}\\
\Phi_\mathrm{fuel} ={}& \pi_\mathrm{f} \sum_t P_\mathrm{rx}(t)\Delta t, \label{eq:fuel_cost}\\
\Phi_\mathrm{grid} ={}&
  \sum_t \pi_\mathrm{g}(t)\big[P_\mathrm{g}^{+}(t) - P_\mathrm{g}^{-}(t)\big]\Delta t, \label{eq:grid_cost}\\
\Phi_\mathrm{CO_2} ={}&
  \pi_\mathrm{CO_2}\Theta \sum_t
  \Big[\gamma_\mathrm{rx} P_\mathrm{tn}(t) + \theta(t)\big(P_\mathrm{g}^{+}(t)-P_\mathrm{g}^{-}(t)\big)\Big]\Delta t. \label{eq:carbon_cost}
\end{align}
The nuclear fuel price $\pi_\mathrm{f}$ is charged on thermal energy because extraction does not reduce fuel burn, but it is anchored on the net-electric basis the fleet statistics use: $\pi_\mathrm{f} = \pi_\mathrm{f}^\mathrm{e}\,(\overline{P}_\mathrm{tn}/\overline{P}_\mathrm{rx})$ with $\pi_\mathrm{f}^\mathrm{e} = \$10~\mathrm{MWh}_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ \citep{li2026nuclearH2}, i.e., \$3.10~MWh$_\mathrm{th}^{-1}$ at the plant's 31\% net efficiency. For Case~3, the same accounting buckets are used but the reactor terms are replaced by the NGCC terms. The combined-cycle heat rate rises below the design point, so fuel use and combustion emissions are both scaled by a part-load multiplier $\phi_\mathrm{ng}(\ell)\ge 1$ of the electrical load fraction $\ell(t)=P_\mathrm{ng}(t)/\overline{P}_\mathrm{ng}$, taken from a representative F-class combined-cycle characteristic extended below the 40\% gas-turbine stable-load point with the steeper low-load branch of published combined-cycle data, and equal to one at full load. The VOM term uses $v_\mathrm{ng}P_\mathrm{ng}(t)+v_\mathrm{v}Q_\mathrm{v}(t)$, where $P_\mathrm{ng}(t)$ is the NGCC electric output required by the IT and VCC loads, and the fuel and carbon terms become
\begin{align}
\Phi_\mathrm{fuel}^\mathrm{ng} ={}& \sum_t \pi_\mathrm{ng}(t)\,h_\mathrm{ng}\,\phi_\mathrm{ng}\!\big(\ell(t)\big)\,P_\mathrm{ng}(t)\Delta t, \label{eq:ngcc_fuel_cost}\\
\Phi_\mathrm{CO_2}^\mathrm{ng} ={}&
  \pi_\mathrm{CO_2}\Theta \sum_t
  \gamma_\mathrm{ng}\,\phi_\mathrm{ng}\!\big(\ell(t)\big)\, P_\mathrm{ng}(t)\Delta t, \label{eq:ngcc_carbon_cost}
\end{align}
with full-load heat rate $h_\mathrm{ng}=3.412/\eta_\mathrm{hhv}$~MMBtu~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ and full-load lifecycle factor $\gamma_\mathrm{ng}=420$~g~CO$_2$-eq~kWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$, equal to 360 direct-combustion and 60 upstream-methane g~CO$_2$-eq~kWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ \citep{alvarez2018methane}. Applying the same $\phi_\mathrm{ng}$ to both terms holds the fuel carbon content constant. The carbon term $\Phi_\mathrm{CO_2}$ multiplies the case carbon footprint by a uniform price $\pi_\mathrm{CO_2}$ and a unit-conversion factor $\Theta=10^{-3}$ t~CO$_2$~kg$^{-1}$. Exports credit the case at the ERCOT hourly average carbon intensity $\theta(t)$, derived from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA)-930 generation mix \citep{eia930}, to reflect the time-varying emissions of the grid power exchanged, following time-resolved hourly emissions accounting rather than a static annual-average factor \citep{li2025ghgDemand}; this hourly average emission factor is used as a tractable proxy for the true marginal emission factor \citep{hawkes2010marginal,silerEvans2013regional}. $\mathrm{CRF}_k=i(1+i)^{N_k}/[(1+i)^{N_k}-1]$ is evaluated at the asset lives listed above and the baseline WACC $i=6.7\%$; the battery additionally carries a 5\% end-of-life salvage credit. The 6.7\% rate is the NLR ATB 2024 nominal financing assumption \citep{atb2024nuclear}, applied here to price and cost series held in constant dollars; this pairing overstates capital recovery relative to a fully real treatment (the implied real rate is about 4.4\% at the assumed 2.2\% inflation), so it is conservative for the capital-dominated nuclear cases. The WACC sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:results:wacc}) sweeps $i$ over $\{5\%, 6.7\%, 10\%\}$.

\runinhead{Clean-electricity production tax credit}
The nuclear cases earn the technology-neutral clean-electricity production tax credit (Section~45Y; 26~U.S.C.~\S\,45Y), enacted in the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 \citep{ira2022}, on their generation \citep{usc26_45y,irs45y}: a new-build zero-emission reactor placed in service after 2024 qualifies, unlike under the legacy Section~45U credit, which was restricted to reactors already operating before the Act. At the prevailing-wage rate the inflation-adjusted credit for calendar 2025 is $\bar{c}=\$30~\mathrm{MWh}_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ (3.0\textcent~kWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$) \citep{fr2025_45y}, flat in the market price with no gross-receipts phaseout, and payable for the statutory ten-year period from commissioning. Because the TAC compares costs over a twenty-year horizon, the credit is levelized across that window by the ratio of annuity present-value factors,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ptc}
c_{45\mathrm{Y}} \;=\; \bar{c}\,\frac{A(i,10)}{A(i,20)}, \qquad A(i,n)=\frac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i},
\end{equation}
which gives $c_{45\mathrm{Y}}=\$19.7~\mathrm{MWh}_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ at the baseline $i=6.7\%$. The annual credit is applied to net nuclear generation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ptc_total}
\Phi_\mathrm{ptc} \;=\; c_{45\mathrm{Y}} \sum_t P_\mathrm{tn}(t)\,\Delta t.
\end{equation}
We treat the credit as a baseline policy condition and hold the statutory amount at its calendar-2025 value; the same \$30~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$, ten-year parameterization is used in the companion nuclear--hydrogen study \citep{li2026nuclearH2}. Because the credit is generation-indexed, it raises the opportunity cost of every megawatt-hour the absorber diverts from the turbine, a coupling that shapes the cooling dispatch in Section~\ref{sec:results:cooling}. Cases~0 and 3 carry no nuclear generation and therefore no credit.

The cross-case figure of merit used throughout the rest of the paper is the grid-cost margin
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin}
M_c \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\mathrm{TAC}_c}{\mathrm{TAC}_0}\Big|_{\text{matched scenario}},
\end{equation}
defined as one minus the ratio of the case TAC to the Case~0 (grid-only) TAC at the same year and carbon price. In the cooling-efficiency sweep (group G1), where the swept chiller COP applies to the on-site cases, margins are quoted against the fixed full-load PUE-1.35 grid-only baseline -- the same IT load served by a conventionally cooled facility. A positive margin means the configuration is cheaper than grid procurement and a negative margin means it is more expensive; the magnitude is the relative cost difference.

\label{sec:methods:data}

Inputs to the optimization fall into three families: exogenous time series (hourly traces of IT load, electricity price, average carbon intensity and wet-bulb temperature), equipment parameters (capital and operating cost coefficients plus technical bounds for each plant block) and financial parameters (project lifetime and weighted-average cost of capital). All runs use 8760-hour annual operating horizons; leap-year raw files are trimmed to 8760 hourly records for cross-year comparability. The four characteristic input traces are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:inputs}; aggregated values and source citations are listed in Tables~\ref{tab:data}--\ref{tab:equipment_tech}.

\section{Results and discussion}
\label{sec:results}

The analysis is organized around a single competitiveness metric, the grid-cost margin of Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin}: positive values mean that an on-site option beats grid procurement, negative values mean that it does not. This framing matters because the reactor, cooling system and policy support move the margin in different directions. Reactor capital and financing determine the annualized fixed-cost burden, absorption cooling changes both chiller electricity and turbine output, grid exchange determines merchant-export value, and the Section~45Y PTC offsets eligible nuclear generation. The results below first establish the baseline economic gap, then ask whether operations, cooling-side value, reactor capital, financing, market year or carbon policy can close it.

\subsection{Mid-range reactor capital keeps nuclear options above grid supply}
\label{sec:results:baseline}

At the NLR Annual Technology Baseline mid-range (ATB-Mid) reactor-capital baseline under 2023 market conditions, the nuclear configurations are not at grid parity. The grid-supplied data center (Case~0) has a TAC of \$75.9~M~yr$^{-1}$, of which 93\% is the electricity bill. The two reactor configurations cost \$113.4 and \$122.6~M~yr$^{-1}$ without heat recovery (Case~1) and with absorption cooling (Case~2), respectively, even after the Section~45Y PTC returns \$42.9 and \$41.8~M~yr$^{-1}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_economics}a). Reactor capital recovery dominates both totals. Grid-export revenue and the PTC offset much of the fixed-cost burden, but not enough to prevent grid-cost margins of $-49\%$ and $-62\%$.

The only baseline configuration that beats grid procurement is the on-site NGCC plant (Case~3), at \$55.6~M~yr$^{-1}$ and a $+27\%$ grid-cost margin. This result is tied to the 2023 fuel-price regime: delivered natural gas averaged \$2.63~MMBtu$^{-1}$, while ERCOT day-ahead prices remained high enough for the grid-only bill to exceed on-site fuel cost. Because Case~3 is islanded, it cannot import during the roughly 42\% of hours when the day-ahead price falls below its running cost, so the comparison is an annual cost result rather than an hour-by-hour arbitrage result. Adding absorption cooling to the nuclear plant raises cost by \$9.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ relative to the reactor-only case, showing already at the baseline that heat recovery is not an economic lever at current capital costs.

The BWRX-300 unit-size constraint explains part, but not most, of the baseline nuclear penalty. The smallest commercial unit has a 270~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ net rating, about 2.9 times the data center's 94.7~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ average IT load, so Case~2 has net exports of about 1.14~TWh~yr$^{-1}$ to ERCOT, with 1.22~TWh~yr$^{-1}$ of gross sales against 0.08~TWh~yr$^{-1}$ of grid imports concentrated in the refueling-outage window. Scaling the campus to 2.0$\times$ the baseline load brings the nuclear site close to import-export balance, but the matched Case~2 margin is still $-37\%$; beyond that scale the site becomes a net importer and the margin improves only to $-29\%$ at 3.0$\times$ (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:size_matching} and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:size_matching}). Unit-size matching therefore improves utilization intensity, reducing the Case~2 excess cost from \$56 to \$26~MWh$_\mathrm{IT}^{-1}$ between the baseline and 3.0$\times$, but it does not remove the ATB-Mid reactor-capital gap.

The baseline cost ranking is mirrored in the cost--carbon plane (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_economics}b,c). The nuclear cases occupy the low-carbon region after reactor-lifecycle emissions and grid-export credits are accounted for using the ERCOT hourly average carbon intensity, while the grid-only case anchors the low-cost region in 2023 and 2024. Case~3 trades higher emissions for lower cost under 2023 gas prices. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_economics}c shows that export credits more than offset grid-import and reactor-lifecycle emissions in Cases~1 and 2, but low carbon intensity alone does not make either reactor configuration cost-competitive at zero carbon price and mid-range reactor capital.

Secondary endpoints lead to the same boundary interpretation rather than a different ranking (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:secondary_kpis_cost_carbon} and~\ref{tab:secondary_kpis_water}). At the baseline, Case~2 has a net levelized cost of IT supply (NLCS) of \$147.8~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ against \$91.4~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ for grid procurement, an energy payback time of 0.57~yr and a total water footprint of 6{,}786~L~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$. These metrics show why the low-carbon reactor cases remain expensive at ATB-Mid capital: their net average-emissions footprint is negative under the export-credit convention, but the capital-driven cost and water-footprint penalties remain large.

\subsection{Absorption cooling dispatches against price but adds no net value}
\label{sec:results:cooling}

The hourly dispatch of Case~2 shows that the two chillers split the cooling load along the electricity price rather than the cooling demand (Figure~\ref{fig:operation_value}a,b). Diverting steam to the absorber forfeits the flat Section~45Y credit on the turbine output it displaces, whereas running the electric chiller only costs the prevailing LMP, so the absorber is economic when the hourly price (and the electric chiller's rising part-load marginal draw) is high, and the VCC serves the cheap hours. In the January week, with a mean LMP of \$22~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$, the absorber supplies only about a quarter of the cooling; in the July week, with afternoon prices far above the credit-adjusted threshold, it supplies just under two thirds, retreating only in the hottest afternoon hours, when the wet-bulb derate of the COP toward its 1.10 design value tightens the steam budget. The reactor itself runs at nameplate thermal output through both plotted weeks, and the turbine turns down by no more than about 26~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ from its 270~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ net rating, because the credit keeps every megawatt-hour of generation valuable. Across the full year the absorption chiller delivers 352~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$ (38\% of cooling) over roughly 5{,}300 operating hours, and the VCC 570~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$, including the full cooling load of the 29-day refueling outage, when the absorber has no steam source and its own maintenance is scheduled. The LiBr crystallization gate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:abs_avail} never engages in the 2023 weather record: the 34~$^\circ$C cooling-water shutdown threshold corresponds to a 29~$^\circ$C wet-bulb gate, above the 28~$^\circ$C annual peak.

The waterfall in Figure~\ref{fig:operation_value}c decomposes the \$9.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ gap between Case~2 and Case~1 into the marginal components of adding absorption cooling to the nuclear-only plant (component values in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:value_decomp}). The chiller displaces \$17.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ of gross vapor-compression electricity at the ERCOT 2023 hourly import price. Against this credit stand, in annual terms, \$13.2~M for absorption capital plus fixed operations and maintenance, \$7.5~M for the Willans-line electricity opportunity cost of extracted steam (the marginal turbine-output loss caused by steam diversion, valued at the net salable output), \$3.1~M for the VCC electricity that still serves the cheap-hour and outage cooling, and \$1.1~M for the reduction in the production tax credit, since diverting steam to the absorber lowers the creditable turbine output. A $-\$1.4$~M~yr$^{-1}$ residual of second-order dispatch interactions (absorber parasitics and variable operations and maintenance) reconciles the component sum with the measured TAC difference; the monetized direct site-water increment, which carries no price in the cost objective, is reported as a memo externality of only \$0.01~M~yr$^{-1}$. The avoided electric cooling no longer covers the chiller's capital and steam-opportunity costs, so the penalty is set jointly by the absorption capital and the credit-inflated value of every diverted megawatt-hour.

\subsection{Cooling efficiency moves the absorption case but not the boundary}
\label{sec:results:pue}

With the heat-rejection load fixed at $P_\mathrm{IT}/\eta_\mathrm{ch}$, the cooling-side lever is the efficiency of the competing electric chiller rather than the size of the duty. The cooling-efficiency sweep (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:cooling_response}) varies the chiller COP so that the reported PUE spans $\{1.10, 1.30, 1.50\}$ for Cases~1 and 2, with all other parameters at the 2023 ATB-Mid baseline. The absorber is not forced to run whenever cooling demand exists: at PUE~1.10, highly efficient electric cooling makes steam diversion unattractive in every hour of the year and the absorption chiller never operates, so the gap to Case~1 is exactly the idle absorber's \$13.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ capital and fixed-cost burden. At PUE~1.30 and 1.50 the chiller delivers 193 and 801~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$~yr$^{-1}$, respectively, as the price threshold above which steam diversion pays falls with the efficiency of the displaced electric cooling. Net cooling-electricity saving after the remaining VCC dispatch rises from zero to \$10.8 and \$23.8~M~yr$^{-1}$ across the sweep. As a result, the Case~2 penalty against Case~1 narrows from \$13.2 to \$11.0 and \$2.4~M~yr$^{-1}$, but it never becomes a saving inside the swept range: with the flat Section~45Y credit raising the value of every diverted megawatt-hour, break-even lies just beyond PUE~1.50, where a pre-credit accounting would already sit essentially at parity. The main-results baseline (full-load PUE~1.35) carries a \$9.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ gap.

\subsection{Viability opens only along the low reactor-capital frontier}
\label{sec:results:frontier}

The ERCOT price regime during the year of operation is the largest single market sensitivity (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}a and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:market_years}). The 2022 cluster captures the high natural-gas price regime: delivered natural gas (Henry Hub plus Houston Ship Channel basis) averaged \$6.53~MMBtu$^{-1}$, and the NGCC margin falls to $-2\%$, essentially parity with the grid, because the grid clearing price and the on-site fuel cost moved together. The same high clearing prices lift the merchant value of nuclear exports: the reactor-only Case~1 comes within 14\% of grid parity at mid-range capital, its best market outcome anywhere in the solved envelope. In 2023, low gas prices (\$2.63~MMBtu$^{-1}$) combined with firm ERCOT prices made Case~3 the only on-site option that beat grid supply (a $+27\%$ margin). The 2024 cluster shows the deepest negative margin for every on-site option because ERCOT LMPs settled at a multi-year low, an annual mean of about \$28~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$, making grid procurement comparatively inexpensive: the grid-only TAC falls to \$36.5~M~yr$^{-1}$ and the nuclear margins drop to $-288\%$ (Case~1) and $-322\%$ (Case~2). Across the three years, no on-site option beats grid supply in 2024 or 2022, and only NGCC beats it in 2023. The year regime therefore sets the market boundary on both sides: a $\pm 50\%$ swing in Henry Hub gas price or ERCOT LMP can overturn the grid--NGCC ranking, and the export-revenue swing moves the nuclear margins by some 270--300 percentage points between the 2022 and 2024 regimes, comparable to the reactor capital-cost axis itself.

The battery energy storage system (BESS) axis tests whether a 100~MWh, 50~MW lithium-ion battery changes the competitiveness frontier. At the two-hour utility-scale capital cost of \$529~kWh$^{-1}$ the battery does not pay for itself: it raises TAC by \$0.76~M~yr$^{-1}$ in each nuclear case, a one-percentage-point margin penalty that never alters the configuration ranking (Supplementary Information). Because this effect is much smaller than the market, capital-cost and financing axes, it is retained as a secondary boundary rather than as a main result.

SMR overnight capital cost is the dominant technology-cost axis (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}b). At FOAK (\$14{,}700~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$) the margin is $-232\%$ (Case~1) and $-244\%$ (Case~2): reactor capital outweighs all other costs severalfold, and the production tax credit barely dents the gap. At ATB-Mid the margin settles at $-49\%$ (Case~1) and $-62\%$ (Case~2), so even with the credit, mid-range capital leaves the nuclear cases roughly 1.5--1.6 times the cost of grid supply. At NOAK (\$2{,}250~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$) the picture inverts decisively: the margin rises to $+89\%$ for Case~1 and $+77\%$ for Case~2, and the configurations clear grid parity even before the credit, with pre-credit margins of $+32\%$ and $+22\%$. The \$42.9~M~yr$^{-1}$ Case~1 credit is worth about 56 percentage points of margin at every capital level, so its decisive zone is the middle of the trajectory: it cuts the pre-credit ATB-Mid gap of $-106\%$ roughly in half and pulls the parity threshold down the capital axis toward \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}d). Coupling the NOAK reactor with the absorption chiller (Case~2 NOAK) is marginally less competitive than Case~1 NOAK, because the absorption capital and the credit on diverted generation outweigh the cooling-side savings, but both stay comfortably below grid cost.

\runinhead{Financing sensitivity}
\label{sec:results:wacc}
The financing assumption changes the same capital-dominated boundary rather than creating a separate operating mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_atlas}c). Sweeping the WACC between 5\% and 10\% for Case~2 while holding absorption capital, reactor capital and operating parameters fixed moves TAC from \$94 to \$184~M~yr$^{-1}$, a \$90~M~yr$^{-1}$ range. The implied grid-cost margin ranges from $-24\%$ at 5\% WACC to $-143\%$ at 10\% WACC. By comparison, the full range of group G4 (reactor capital, FOAK to NOAK) moves the Case~2 margin between $-244\%$ and $+77\%$, crossing the parity line. Financing therefore brings the mid-range case within reach of parity at concessional rates but cannot cross it alone. Policy or contracting mechanisms that lower the effective cost of capital for SMR projects, such as long-term offtake agreements, sovereign loan guarantees or accelerated depreciation, compress the gap to grid supply without eliminating it.

Both axes sweep simultaneously: SMR capital cost runs from NOAK to FOAK on the vertical axis (\$2{,}250--14{,}700~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$), and absorption-chiller capital cost runs from Bare-Low to Turnkey-High on the horizontal axis (\$450--1{,}200~kW$_\mathrm{c}^{-1}$). Every cell in the $5 \times 5$ grid is solved at 2023 market conditions, full-load PUE~1.35, WACC 6.7\% and zero carbon price, with the production tax credit applied. The viable region now extends well past the NOAK edge: every cell in the NOAK row reaches a strongly positive grid-cost margin, from $+82\%$ with Bare-Low absorption to $+69\%$ with Turnkey-High, and the Low-Mid reactor row at \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ straddles the parity line, from $+11\%$ at Bare-Low to $-2\%$ at Turnkey-High, so the margin $=0$ contour crosses inside that row. The most favorable corner (NOAK reactor with a Bare-Low absorption chiller at \$450~kW$_\mathrm{c}^{-1}$) lands at $+82\%$, and the least favorable (FOAK reactor with Turnkey-High absorption) at $-252\%$. The frontier is still governed almost entirely by the reactor axis: absorption capital shifts the margin by at most 13 percentage points across its full published range, whereas one step up the reactor axis to the ATB-Mid row drops the margin to $-56$ to $-70\%$. Reaching roughly \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ of reactor capital is therefore the binding condition: with the production tax credit it makes colocated SMR with absorption cooling competitive with grid supply at 2023 ERCOT prices and zero carbon cost, and no tested capital trajectory at or above the ATB-Mid row crosses parity.

\subsection{Carbon pricing closes the remaining mid-range gap}
\label{sec:results:policy}

A carbon price provides a second policy pathway to nuclear competitiveness, complementary to the capital route just described. With capital held at the ATB-Mid baseline, the policy axis is the carbon price (Figure~\ref{fig:policy_sensitivity_summary}a). All four cases have net annual CO$_2$ footprints that are nearly fixed by dispatch (Case~0 $+363$, Case~3 $+486$, Case~1 $-344$ and Case~2 $-360$~kt~CO$_2$~yr$^{-1}$), so each TAC--carbon relationship is essentially linear over the plotted range. The nuclear footprints barely deepen as carbon is valued, because the production tax credit already drives the reactor to export close to its full surplus, leaving little additional export to unlock.

The grid-only Case~0 increases by \$0.36~M~yr$^{-1}$ for each \$1~tCO$_2^{-1}$ rise in carbon price because the ERCOT grid mix is carbon-intensive. The two nuclear cases decrease by about \$0.34--0.37~M~yr$^{-1}$ under the same price increment, because their grid exports displace carbon-intensive generation and receive a corresponding credit. Case~3 NGCC increases by \$0.49~M~yr$^{-1}$ for each \$1~tCO$_2^{-1}$ rise, carrying the full direct emissions on the buyer side. As the carbon price rises, the two on-site options cross the grid-only baseline in opposite directions, and the nuclear crossings fall inside the solved \$0--100~tCO$_2^{-1}$ range: Case~1 reaches grid parity at \$53~tCO$_2^{-1}$ and Case~2 at \$64~tCO$_2^{-1}$, and at the \$100~tCO$_2^{-1}$ solved endpoint they are 30\% and 23\% cheaper than grid supply, respectively. NGCC starts below grid and rises above it near an extrapolated \$165~tCO$_2^{-1}$, beyond the solved range. This carbon-pricing pathway is independent of the capital route: at NOAK capital the nuclear cases already sit far below grid cost at zero carbon price.

The cogeneration cooling earns only a small, indirect carbon benefit: meeting cooling from steam rather than electricity lets Case~2 export slightly more than Case~1, giving it a marginally deeper net footprint ($-360$ versus $-344$~kt~CO$_2$~yr$^{-1}$), but its higher cost still delays its crossover relative to the reactor-only case (\$64 versus \$53~tCO$_2^{-1}$); thermal integration does not create a separate credit mechanism. Both crossings depend on crediting the reactor's merchant exports at the ERCOT hourly average carbon intensity: the nuclear cases reach a net-negative footprint only because the roughly 1.14~TWh~yr$^{-1}$ of net exported electricity is credited as displaced grid generation, and the crossover prices scale directly with how much of that credit a given accounting framework retains. Because export-credit retention is not varied in the solved carbon-price sweep (group G6), this accounting choice is treated as a limitation rather than as an additional solved boundary.

The ranked sensitivity panel in Figure~\ref{fig:policy_sensitivity_summary}b summarizes the hierarchy of boundary axes. Around the Case~2 ATB-Mid baseline ($-62\%$), the full one-axis span is 321 percentage points for SMR capital cost ($-244$ to $+77\%$) and 297 percentage points for market year ($-322$ to $-25\%$). WACC contributes a 119-percentage-point span, while carbon price and data-center size contribute 85 and 82 percentage points, respectively. Absorption capital, PUE and BESS deployment are much smaller axes at 13, 12 and 1 percentage points. Thus the global ranking in the solved envelope is consistent with the one-dimensional results: reactor capital and market regime determine the boundary, carbon pricing can carry the mid-range case to parity (financing narrows the gap but does not close it), and cooling-side refinements do not overturn the capital-cost ordering.

\subsection{Boundary synthesis and limitations}
\label{sec:results:synthesis}

Across the four configurations and 100 model runs, the consistent boundary is not whether heat recovery can technically serve the cooling load, but whether reactor capital cost has fallen far enough for the Section~45Y clean-electricity production tax credit to close the remaining gap to grid procurement. The mechanism is a fixed-cost threshold rather than an operating-efficiency threshold. Sweeping reactor and absorption-chiller capital together, the entire NOAK reactor row is competitive, from $+82\%$ with a Bare-Low absorption chiller to $+69\%$ with a Turnkey-High one, and the Low-Mid reactor row at \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ straddles parity. Absorption capital changes the margin by at most 13 percentage points over its published range, whereas the reactor-capital trajectory moves the margin by hundreds of percentage points, and the credit shifts every nuclear margin by a constant $\sim$56 percentage points, so the credit is decisive exactly where the pre-credit gap is comparable to its own value -- the middle of the capital trajectory.

This finding reframes the data-center nuclear question. Unit-size matching removes an important distortion but does not create parity: scaling the load to 2.0$\times$ brings the reactor close to import-export balance, yet the cogeneration plant remains 37\% more expensive than the matched grid baseline. Market year matters on the nuclear side as well as the gas side: high-price years lift merchant-export value enough to carry the reactor-only case within 14\% of parity, while a cheap-grid year such as 2024 pushes it hundreds of points away. Financing helps by lowering annualized fixed cost, and at a 5\% WACC the mid-range Case~2 comes within 24\% of grid supply, but the sweep does not cross parity on its own. Carbon pricing is a quantitatively credible second pathway: with reactor capital held at the mid-range baseline, the nuclear cases cross the grid-only baseline at \$53--64~tCO$_2^{-1}$, inside the solved range, and are 23--30\% cheaper than grid supply at the \$100~tCO$_2^{-1}$ endpoint. The low-capital route remains the more robust of the two because it does not depend on the export-credit convention at all.

Against this capital-dominated boundary, cooling integration should be interpreted as a price-responsive dispatch asset rather than the main economic source of value. The absorption chiller supplies 352~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$~yr$^{-1}$, about 38\% of annual cooling, concentrating its operation in the hours when electricity is expensive, but it raises cost by \$9.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ at the baseline because its capital cost, the lost turbine output and the production tax credit forfeited on diverted steam exceed the avoided electric cooling cost (Section~\ref{sec:results:cooling}). The penalty shrinks as the competing electric chiller becomes less efficient, but break-even lies beyond a full-load PUE of 1.50 in the swept range, and a flat generation credit structurally disadvantages steam diversion. Absorption can still matter for inefficient campuses or carbon-constrained settings, where the margin gap narrows with carbon price. This refines waste-heat studies that treat absorption as categorically beneficial and reactor techno-economic studies that omit cooling-side physics or generation-indexed policy support.

\runinhead{Limitations}
\label{sec:results:limitations}
Several limitations bound the quantitative margins without overturning the central ordering. The load-scaling sweep uses one hyperscale profile, so campuses dominated by different artificial-intelligence workloads or located in different climates could shift the cooling value. The carbon-policy route uses hourly average rather than marginal emission factors, so a merit-order marginal series would better estimate displaced emissions from nuclear exports, and the route is correspondingly sensitive to the export-credit convention. Demand charges and ancillary-service revenues are not modeled; restoring the full delivered grid cost, including transmission, ancillary and demand charges, would raise the Case~0 baseline and narrow every on-site margin, so the penalties reported here are conservative against the on-site configurations. The gas comparator omits forced outages, redundant generation and an outage-covering grid tie, so five-nines availability requirements would erode its baseline advantage; the nuclear cases carry only the scheduled refueling outage, so forced-outage exposure would likewise raise their grid-backup cost. The production tax credit is applied at the calendar-2025 prevailing-wage value of \$30~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ for its statutory ten-year window, levelized across the twenty-year comparison horizon, so construction-start timing, prevailing-wage compliance and future inflation adjustments would shift the credited value. The reactor is also treated as the smallest deployable commercial unit rather than an optimized multi-module buildout. All of these should be evaluated with region-specific tariff, market-service and marginal-emissions data before procurement.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Colocated small modular reactors with absorption cooling are a conditional, not categorical, low-carbon strategy for hyperscale data centers. Across the four configurations and 100 annual hourly scenario runs -- spanning data-center size, three ERCOT years, three SMR capital-cost trajectories, a $5\times5$ joint reactor--absorption capital-cost grid, three carbon prices and three WACC levels, with the Section~45Y production tax credit applied to the nuclear cases -- the consistent boundary is set by reactor capital cost combined with the credit, not by heat recovery. At 2023 ATB-Mid prices for the baseline 200~MW$_\mathrm{e}$ campus the cogeneration configuration is 62\% more expensive than grid supply even with the credit, and \$9.2~M~yr$^{-1}$ more expensive than a reactor-only plant. At first-of-a-kind and mid-range capital the SMR cases remain far above grid. At nth-of-a-kind capital the credit brings both below grid cost, by 89\% (Case~1) and 77\% (Case~2) with no carbon price; the central conclusion is therefore that nuclear data-center cogeneration becomes viable once reactor capital falls to roughly \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ on the learning trajectory between the mid-range and nth-of-a-kind anchors.

Carbon pricing is a quantitatively credible second pathway, closing the mid-range gap at \$53--64~tCO$_2^{-1}$ inside the solved range, though it remains convention-bound because the crossings credit merchant exports at the hourly average emission factor. Absorption cooling is a price-responsive dispatch asset rather than the source of value: it supplies 38\% of annual cooling but raises cost at the baseline, and break-even against the electric chiller lies just beyond a full-load PUE of 1.50 under the generation-indexed credit. For procurement, the result is a short list of conditions that must coincide: reactor capital at or below roughly \$5{,}000~kW$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$, clean-electricity production-tax-credit eligibility, a load large enough to use the smallest commercial reactor and a market regime in which grid procurement is not unusually cheap. Where those conditions hold, SMR cogeneration can undercut grid supply without a carbon price, and absorption cooling can be chosen for operational reasons rather than as the source of the saving. The broader contribution is the boundary map itself, which provides a way to test nuclear data-center proposals against region-specific tariffs, financing, cooling climates and carbon-accounting rules before treating firm low-carbon supply as an economic proposition. The scenario envelope and input provenance documented in the Supplementary Information provide the basis for extending the analysis to other ISO regions, climates and data-center sizes.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note 4: Cogeneration dispatch and storage detail}\phantomsection\label{note:dispatch}
In the 2023 ATB-Mid baseline, the absorption chiller delivers 352~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$ of cooling (38\% of the annual duty) over roughly 5{,}300 operating hours, and the VCC 570~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$. The split follows the hourly price: diverting steam forfeits the levelized Section~45Y credit on displaced generation, so the absorber runs when the LMP and the electric chiller's part-load marginal draw are high, and the electric chiller serves the cheap hours. During the 701-hour refueling outage the reactor and absorber are both offline -- the absorber's annual maintenance is scheduled inside the window -- and the site imports an average of 121~MW of grid power while the electric chiller carries 73~GWh$_\mathrm{c}$ of cooling. The crystallization gate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:abs_avail} never engages in the 2023 weather record. In the islanded gas case (Case~3) the 2023 day-ahead price falls below the NGCC running cost of about \$21~MWh$_\mathrm{e}^{-1}$ in roughly 42\% of hours, but the disconnected plant cannot import in those hours, so its comparison against grid supply is made on annual cost rather than hour by hour. The lithium-ion battery sweep (group G3) raises total annualized cost by \$0.76~M~yr$^{-1}$ in each nuclear case at the two-hour utility-scale capital cost of \$529~kWh$^{-1}$ -- the arbitrage value no longer covers the battery's annualized capital -- a one-percentage-point margin penalty that never changes the configuration ranking, so storage is reported as a secondary boundary rather than a main result.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note 5: Market inputs by operating year}\phantomsection\label{note:market}
The three modeled years bracket the recent range of ERCOT and natural-gas market conditions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:market_years}). The table documents why 2023 is the baseline operating year: 2022 has high annual-mean gas and high annual-mean LMP, 2023 has moderate annual means with low gas but firm power prices, and 2024 has low annual-mean LMP that makes grid procurement comparatively cheap. The labels are annual-mean market descriptors and do not imply that 2023 lacked short-duration price spikes. The on-site NGCC (Case~3) beats grid supply only in 2023, when low delivered gas coincides with firm ERCOT prices; in 2022 the gas and grid prices move together and erase the advantage, and in 2024 the low ERCOT price makes grid procurement the cheapest option.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note 7: Exogenous data sources and provenance}\phantomsection\label{note:provenance}
Each exogenous input is drawn from a public, citable source and trimmed to an 8760-hour annual horizon. ERCOT day-ahead locational marginal prices at the Houston Hub (HB\_HOUSTON) are retrieved through the gridstatus.io public data interface \citep{gridstatus}. Hourly average grid carbon intensities are derived from the U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA-930 Hourly Electric Grid Monitor generation mix \citep{eia930} weighted by United Nations Economic Commission for Europe 2022 lifecycle emission factors for each technology \citep{unece2022lca}. Houston wet-bulb temperature is taken from the Open-Meteo historical-weather reanalysis archive \citep{openmeteo2023}. The IT-load trace is derived from the Vercellino et al.\ 2026 NLR Data Catalog colocation profile, scaling the 10~MW source profile (measured in calendar 2018) by 20$\times$ to represent a 200~MW facility and circularly shifting it by whole days to align day-of-week with each market year \citep{vercellino2026aiworkload}. Delivered natural gas is the U.S. Energy Information Administration Henry Hub daily spot price \citep{eiaHenryHub} plus a fixed Houston Ship Channel basis. The ATB-Mid reactor capital anchor, the NGCC cost block and the reactor and NGCC fixed operations and maintenance follow the National Laboratory of the Rockies 2024 Annual Technology Baseline \citep{atb2024nuclear}, while the FOAK and NOAK reactor anchors are the Ontario Power Generation Darlington unit budget (SMR-unit-only basis) and the GE-Hitachi vendor target, respectively; the reactor capacity factor is the U.S. Energy Information Administration fleet average \citep{eia2025capacityfactors}; the nuclear fuel cost follows the companion nuclear--hydrogen study \citep{li2026nuclearH2}; and absorption-chiller capital costs are bracketed from U.S. Department of Energy combined-heat-and-power fact-sheet data \citep{doe2017chpAbsorption} and publicly available vendor literature. The battery capital cost follows the NLR 2024 Annual Technology Baseline utility-scale battery storage case \citep{atb2024nuclear}, and the vapor-compression chiller capital cost is a typical water-cooled centrifugal value from ASHRAE engineering practice. The Section~45Y clean-electricity production tax credit follows the enacting statute \citep{usc26_45y,ira2022}, the calendar-2025 inflation adjustment \citep{fr2025_45y} and U.S. Internal Revenue Service guidance \citep{irs45y}. No proprietary or restricted-access data are used, and the audit trail linking each raw file to its processed model input is included in the project repository.
